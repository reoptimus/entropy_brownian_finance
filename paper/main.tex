\begin{abstract}
\noindent Entropy-based crisis indicators jump in crises and decay slowly
afterwards, and this sawtooth is often read as the trace of information that
arrives in bursts and is absorbed gradually. We show that most of this
signature is a measurement artefact. We decompose the entropy of a return
vector into three channels --- scale, dependence ($\tfrac12\log\det R$) and
shape (the entropy deficit $\DD=\KL{p}{q}$) --- and track the scale-free
structural index $\JJ=\DD-\tfrac12\log\det R=\KL{p}{\prod_i q_i}\ge0$ on twenty
S\&P~500 stocks (1990--2022) and on Kenneth French's 48 industry portfolios
(1990--2026). Every statistic is judged against two calibrated nulls, two
hundred replications each, that resample the observed returns: one i.i.d., one
in 21-day blocks. Jump asymmetry, the skewness signature, channel coupling, the
volatility--dependence offset and the regime gap of $\JJ$ itself are all
reproduced, sometimes exceeded, by resampled data: a positive convex functional
of estimated moments jumps up as soon as a fat-tailed observation enters the
estimator. A single result clears both nulls on both panels: under stress, the
cross-section couples. The regime gap of the dependence channel is
$-0.99$~nat against $-0.21$ and $-0.51$ under the nulls (stocks), and $-3.65$
against $-0.56$ and $-2.46$ (industries). The relaxation half-life is not
identified, and no channel improves out-of-sample risk forecasts. Finally we
present a Kullback--Leibler-ball stress-testing construction whose radius is
calibrated on the market's realised information flow; it prices classical
scenarios in nats, but its severity scale is milder than history, so its return
periods are not validated.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Two facts about financial prices sit uneasily together. The first is that the
Gaussian model is the natural answer to a question of ignorance: fix a mean
and a variance, know nothing else, and the maximum-entropy distribution is
Gaussian. The second is that returns are not Gaussian: they have fat tails,
negative skewness, and their dependence tightens precisely when it matters.

The usual reaction is to treat the second fact as a defect of the first model
and to patch it: add jumps to the price, add a stochastic volatility factor,
add a copula. This paper goes the other way. If the Gaussian is what maximum
entropy delivers, then any departure from the Gaussian measures the market's
distance from maximal ignorance, that is, it measures information. The question
is then not how to patch the Gaussian but how to read the size, the dynamics
and the direction of the departure.

The paper is organised around an asymmetry in that departure. Information
arrives in bursts and is absorbed gradually. A central-bank surprise, a
default, a war removes uncertainty in an instant; the market then spends weeks
working out the implications, and uncertainty rebuilds. If entropy is the right
currency of uncertainty, its dynamics should be a sawtooth: sharp drops, slow
recoveries. That shape is not a stylised fact that has to be assumed --- half of
it is a theorem. Conditioning cannot increase expected entropy, so the arrival
of a signal lowers the \emph{average} conditional entropy by exactly the mutual
information between signal and price (Proposition~\ref{prop:conditioning});
and the maximum-entropy ceiling weakly falls at every realisation, without the
fall being quantifiable in general (Proposition~\ref{prop:constraint}). The two
statements are distinct and Appendix~\ref{app:mi} separates them explicitly,
with a worked Gaussian example. The \emph{expected} size of the jump, in nats,
is the information content of the news. What has to be postulated is the
recovery and the direction.

Three contributions follow.

\paragraph{The volume bridge (Section~\ref{sec:bridge}).}
The step from ``maximum entropy gives a Gaussian'' to ``prices are Gaussian''
is often asserted and rarely argued, because maximum entropy is a statement
about a distribution at fixed variance and says nothing about where that
variance comes from. We close it by defining the price as accumulated order
flow and applying the principle in volume time: total traded volume, which is
observable, fixes the variance budget, and the maximum-entropy increment
conditional on volume is Gaussian with variance proportional to volume. This is
the mixture-of-distributions hypothesis \citep{clark1973,tauchen1983,ane2000},
derived rather than assumed. Temporal consistency then makes the log-price a
Brownian motion subordinated to the volume clock, and It\^o's lemma makes the
price a geometric Brownian motion. The bridge also explains, in the same
sentence, why calendar-time returns are not Gaussian: either the conditional
distribution is not maximum-entropy (information constrains), or the volume
clock is random, or both --- and the two sources leave different fingerprints.

\paragraph{Three channels and a scale-free index (Section~\ref{sec:channels}).}
The Gaussian entropy of a return vector splits exactly into a scale term and a
dependence term, and the true entropy differs from it by the entropy deficit
$\DD=\KL{p}{q}$, where skewness and fat tails live. The scale term dominates the
level of differential entropy and says nothing about information; removing it
leaves $\JJ = \DD - \tfrac12\log\det R$, which is exactly $\KL{p}{\prod_i q_i}$
--- the divergence from a product of Gaussian marginals with the same scales.
$\JJ$ is positive, invariant to the level of volatility, and additive over its
two entropy-destroying channels. It is the state variable of the rest of the
paper. Empirically it shares only $21\%$ of its variance with volatility, and
its shape channel is essentially orthogonal to volatility ($r=0.09$).

\paragraph{Stress tests in nats (Section~\ref{sec:stress}).}
A scenario of severity $\eta$ is the worst conditional distribution inside a KL
ball of radius $\eta$ around today's. The construction is not new
\citep{glasserman2014,breuer2013}; the calibration is. The radius is read off
the market's realised information flow, the $h$-step revision
$\KL{p_t}{p_{t-h}}$, measured in the same unit as the radius, so that a severity
acquires a return period in principle; Section~\ref{sec:calib} shows that on our
data this return period is not validated. Because the mean shift and the
variance inflation are chosen from one budget, the scenario cannot be
internally inconsistent, and because \emph{any} scenario can be priced on the
same scale, the question ``is this stress test severe enough?'' becomes
arithmetic.

\paragraph{An exploratory extension (Appendix~\ref{app:explo}).}
The KL ball fixes the severity of the \emph{portfolio} and is silent on how the
underlying covariance should deform, asset by asset, to reach it. An eighth
hypothesis, which clears only the i.i.d.\ null, is used to calibrate that split
on an exploratory basis; we put it in an appendix so that it does not weigh on
the main conclusions.

\paragraph{A warning about measuring any of this.}
The structural index is a positive convex functional of estimated second, third
and fourth moments. Estimation noise on fat-tailed data pushes such a
functional \emph{up} sharply and lets it decay \emph{smoothly}, which is exactly
the sawtooth the postulates predict. Every jump and relaxation statistic below
is therefore reported against a null built by resampling the observed returns
--- keeping the marginal tails, destroying the chronology. The result is
uncomfortable and, we think, the most useful thing in the paper: the jump
asymmetry, which looks overwhelming, does not survive, nor does the relaxation
half-life; the coupling of the cross-section under stress and the amplitude of
$\JJ$ do. Anyone building a crisis indicator out of a convex functional of
estimated moments should run this null before believing their own chart.

\subsection{Position in the literature}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{What is not new.} None of the mathematical ingredients is, and we
prefer to say so up front. Deriving price dynamics from an entropic inference
principle, rather than assuming them, is the programme of ``entropic
dynamics'', applied to stocks and options \citep{abedi2019} and then to
jump-diffusion processes \citep{abedi2026}; the volume bridge of
Section~\ref{sec:bridge} only adds the volume clock of \citet{clark1973} and
\citet{ane2000}. The decomposition of Section~\ref{sec:channels} is the
classical identity of independent component analysis \citep{comon1994}: the
divergence from a product of Gaussian marginals splits into marginal
negentropies and multi-information, and $-\tfrac12\log\det R$ is the total
correlation of \citet{watanabe1960} of a Gaussian vector. ``Conditioning
reduces entropy'' is a textbook fact \citep{cover2006}. We claim only the
assembly of these pieces into a scale-free index and the testing discipline
that follows.

\paragraph{Crisis indicators built on the correlation structure.} The only
result that survives our nulls --- the cross-section couples under stress ---
is the best-established stylised fact of the crisis literature: correlations
rise when markets fall \citep{longin2001}. It is measured by the variance share
of the leading eigenmode \citep[the ``absorption ratio'' of][]{kritzman2011},
by principal-component and Granger-causality connectedness measures
\citep{billio2012}, by spectral or centrality entropies of the correlation
matrix \citep{almog2019,chakraborti2019,kukreti2020}, by a complexity gap from
random matrix theory \citep{mukhia2026}, by entropy-based early-warning
indicators \citep{billio2016}, and, in central-bank practice, by composite
stress indices \citep{hollo2012}. Our dependence channel $\tfrac12\log\det R$ is
a member of this family, expressed in nats; we do not claim that it dominates
the absorption ratio, and the head-to-head comparison remains to be done. It
also inherits a known problem: under heteroskedasticity, correlation measured
on a high-volatility subsample is mechanically higher, even at constant
underlying correlation \citep{forbes2002}. Section~\ref{sec:forbes} says how
far our nulls address it.

\paragraph{Entropy and crises, without a null.} A large literature reports that
measures of entropy, mutual information or divergence move during crises
\citep{zhou2013,caraiani2018,papla2024,noguer2025}. To our knowledge these
studies compare the measure to zero or to a calm period, rarely to a resampling
of the same returns. Surrogate-data tests are nevertheless the norm in
nonlinear time-series analysis \citep{theiler1992,schreiber2000}. The main
contribution of this paper is to apply that discipline to an entropy-based
crisis indicator and to publish the result, which is largely negative.

\paragraph{Entropic stress tests.} Searching for the worst scenario inside a
Kullback--Leibler ball is the construction of \citet{breuer2013}, close to the
plausibility measure of \citet{breuer2009}, to the minimum-relative-entropy
updating of \citet{meucci2008} and to the robust risk measure of
\citet{glasserman2014}; \citet{glasserman2015} select scenarios by empirical
likelihood. What we add is the calibration of the radius on the market's
realised information flow, and the pricing in nats of existing scenarios.
Section~\ref{sec:calib} shows that this calibration is too mild against
history.

\paragraph{Outline.} Section~\ref{sec:dynamics} states the theorem and the two
postulates and derives the jump-diffusion dynamics;
Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses} lists the hypotheses;
Sections~\ref{sec:data}--\ref{sec:results} give the data, the estimators, the
nulls and the results; Section~\ref{sec:stress} builds the stress test;
Section~\ref{sec:limits} is candid about what none of this establishes.
Appendix~\ref{app:explo} gathers the eighth hypothesis and the calibrated
composition of scenarios, as exploratory material.

\section{From order flow to a Gaussian price: the volume bridge}
\label{sec:bridge}

\subsection{The price as accumulated order flow}

Over $[t,t+\Delta]$, let $M$ be the number of executed trades, trade $k$ carrying
signed volume $\epsilon_k v_k$ with $\epsilon_k\in\{-1,+1\}$ its sign (buyer- or
seller-initiated) and $v_k>0$ its size. Write $V=\sum_k v_k$ for total volume
and $Q=\sum_k \epsilon_k v_k$ for net signed order flow.

\begin{assumption}[Linear impact]\label{as:impact}
The log-price responds linearly to net signed order flow,
$\Delta X_{t,\Delta} = X_{t+\Delta}-X_t = \lambda\,Q$, with $\lambda>0$ constant
over the interval.
\end{assumption}

This is the equilibrium of \citet{kyle1985}: the market maker, unable to tell
informed from uninformed flow, sets a linear pricing rule and $\lambda$ is the
inverse of depth. It is the definition of ``price as a function of traded
volume'' that the rest of the section needs, and it is the weakest link of the
chain; Section~\ref{sec:bridge-caveats} says what happens when it breaks.

\begin{assumption}[Exchangeable signs and variance proportional to volume]
\label{as:signs}
Conditional on $V$ and on the information available at $t$, the market's belief
about $Q$ is constrained only by its first two moments, with
$\mathbb{E}[Q\mid V] = m$ and $\mathrm{Var}(Q\mid V) = \varsigma^2 V$.
\end{assumption}

The form of the variance is what one obtains when trade signs are exchangeable
with bounded correlation and sizes have a stable mean: for independent signs,
$\mathrm{Var}(Q)=\sum_k v_k^2 \approx \bar v V$. The economic content is that
\emph{volume, not time, is the natural measure of what can happen}.

\subsection{Maximum entropy in volume time}

\begin{proposition}[Maximum entropy under first- and second-moment constraints]
\label{prop:maxent}
Among absolutely continuous distributions on $\mathbb{R}^N$ with mean $\mu$ and
covariance $\Sigma\succ0$, the Gaussian $\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)$ uniquely
maximises differential entropy, and
$h^{\max}(\Sigma)=\tfrac12\log\left((2\pi e)^N\det\Sigma\right)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $q=\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)$ and let $p$ have the same first two moments and
finite differential entropy. Since $\log q$ is exactly quadratic, its
expectation depends only on those moments, so
$\mathbb{E}_p[\log q]=\mathbb{E}_q[\log q]=-h(q)$. Positivity of relative entropy
gives $0\le \KL{p}{q} = -h(p)+h(q)$, hence $h(p)\le h(q)$, with equality if and
only if $p=q$ almost everywhere. The argument requires neither differentiability
of $p$ nor the prior existence of a maximiser.
\end{proof}

The variational route --- a Lagrangian on the normalisation, mean and covariance
constraints, whose first-order condition makes $\log p$ quadratic --- explains
\emph{why} the maximiser is Gaussian, but it is the divergence argument that
establishes optimality and uniqueness.

Applying Proposition~\ref{prop:maxent} to Assumption~\ref{as:signs} in one
dimension, then transporting through Assumption~\ref{as:impact}:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mdh}
\Delta X_{t,\Delta}\ \big|\ V \ \sim\ \mathcal{N}\!\left(\lambda m,\ \lambda^2\varsigma^2 V\right).
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:mdh} is the bridge. Three points deserve emphasis.

First, \emph{the conditioning variable is volume, not time}. Maximum entropy
does not deliver a variance; it delivers the least committal distribution given
a variance. Assumption~\ref{as:signs} supplies that variance from an
observable, and that observable is traded volume. Equation~\eqref{eq:mdh} is the
mixture-of-distributions hypothesis of \citet{clark1973} and
\citet{tauchen1983}, obtained here as a maximum-entropy statement rather than
posited; \citet{ane2000} show empirically that returns measured in transaction
time are close to Gaussian while calendar-time returns are not, which is the
prediction of~\eqref{eq:mdh}.

Second, \emph{Gaussianity is conditional}. Unconditionally, $\Delta X$ is a
scale mixture of normals over the random volume $V$, hence leptokurtic even if
\eqref{eq:mdh} holds exactly. This is the subordination mechanism of
\citet{mandelbrot1967}, and it means that fat tails alone are no evidence
against maximum entropy.

Third, the multivariate extension is immediate: with $N$ assets,
$\Delta \mathbf{X}\mid V \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu V, \Sigma V)$, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:maxent} applies as is.

\subsection{From Gaussian increments to Brownian motion and to GBM}

Let $\theta_t=\int_0^t v_s\,ds$ be the cumulative volume clock.
Equation~\eqref{eq:mdh} gives the law of one increment; a process requires more.

\begin{assumption}[Temporal consistency]\label{as:time}
In the volume clock, increments over disjoint intervals are independent, paths
are continuous, and the covariance of an increment is proportional to elapsed
volume.
\end{assumption}

Under Assumption~\ref{as:time} with constant coefficients, there is a matrix $B$
with $BB^{\!\top}=\Sigma$ and $X_\theta = X_0 + \mu\theta + BW_\theta$ in volume
time, hence in calendar time
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:subordination}
X_t = X_0 + \mu\,\theta_t + B\,W_{\theta_t},
\end{equation}
a Brownian motion \emph{subordinated} to the volume clock. If trading intensity
is constant, $\theta_t=\bar v\,t$ and \eqref{eq:subordination} is an ordinary
Brownian motion in calendar time; exponentiating and applying It\^o,
\begin{equation}
\frac{dS_{i,t}}{S_{i,t}} = \left(a_{i,t}+\tfrac12\sigma_{i,t}^2\right)dt + \sigma_{i,t}\,dW_{i,t},
\end{equation}
so that the drift of the price is not that of the log-price --- the same
second-order correction that reappears in Section~\ref{sec:channels} when
$\log\det R_t$ is differentiated.

It is worth being precise about what Assumption~\ref{as:time} does. By the
theorem of \citet{monroe1978}, \emph{every} continuous local martingale is a
time-changed Brownian motion: subordination alone therefore has no empirical
content. The content is in the clock: \eqref{eq:mdh} says the right clock is
volume, and Assumption~\ref{as:time} says the market is memoryless in that
clock. It is maximum entropy that selects Brownian motion within the class that
Monroe's theorem allows.

\subsection{The contrapositive}
\label{sec:contrapositive}

Proposition~\ref{prop:maxent} reads both ways. Let $q_t$ be the Gaussian with
the same conditional mean and covariance as $p_t$. Then

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:deficit}
\DD_t \;\equiv\; h(q_t) - h(p_t) \;=\; \KL{p_t}{q_t} \;\ge\; 0,
\end{equation}
with equality if and only if $p_t$ is Gaussian. Hence: \emph{prices are
non-Gaussian exactly to the extent that their conditional distribution is not
maximum-entropy}, and $\DD_t$ measures that extent. In the language of
Section~\ref{sec:bridge}, a positive $\DD_t$ means that constraints beyond the
first two moments are active --- that someone knows something the second moment
does not encode.

Two sources produce a positive deficit, and separating them matters. A random
volume clock produces a scale mixture, symmetric and leptokurtic. Binding
directional information produces skewness. Section~\ref{sec:channels} splits
$\DD$ exactly along that line.

\subsection{Where the bridge is weak}
\label{sec:bridge-caveats}

Assumption~\ref{as:impact} is linear; empirical metaorder impact is closer to a
square root of size, which makes the map from flow to price concave and the
induced return distribution less fat-tailed than the flow.
Assumption~\ref{as:signs} treats signs as exchangeable; order flow is in fact
strongly autocorrelated, which inflates $\mathrm{Var}(Q)$ above $\varsigma^2 V$
and makes the volume clock an imperfect proxy for variance. None of these
failures changes the \emph{form} of the argument --- maximum entropy under a
variance budget remains Gaussian --- but both mean that the volume clock is an
approximation to the information clock rather than that clock itself. We use
volume-time reasoning to motivate the framework and calendar-time estimation in
all the empirical work, so that no result below depends on the exactness of
\eqref{eq:mdh}.

\section{The three channels of entropy}
\label{sec:channels}

\subsection{Scale and dependence}

Write $\Sigma_t = D_tR_tD_t$ with
$D_t=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1,t},\dots,\sigma_{N,t})$. Then
$\det\Sigma_t = \left(\prod_i\sigma_{i,t}^2\right)\det R_t$ and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ceiling}
h(q_t) \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac{N}{2}\log(2\pi e)}_{\text{constant}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\sum_i \log\sigma_{i,t}}_{H^{vol}_t}
\;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac12\log\det R_t}_{H^{dep}_t}.
\end{equation}
By Hadamard's inequality, $H^{dep}_t\le 0$, with equality if and only if
$R_t=I$: \emph{correlation always destroys entropy}, and $-H^{dep}_t$ is
precisely the Gaussian multi-information (total correlation) of the return
vector. A useful reading of the same quantity is spectral: with $\lambda_k$ the
eigenvalues of $R_t$ and $\tilde\lambda_k=\lambda_k/N$, the participation number
$N^{\text{eff}}_t=\exp\!\left(-\sum_k\tilde\lambda_k\log\tilde\lambda_k\right)$
is the effective number of independent risk modes; it equals $N$ for $R=I$ and
tends to $1$ when a single collective mode absorbs the variance.

Equation~\eqref{eq:ceiling} is the ceiling, not the entropy. It is what the
covariance matrix alone implies, and Proposition~\ref{prop:maxent} says the
truth lies below it.

\subsection{Shape: the entropy deficit and its composition}

Subtracting \eqref{eq:deficit} from \eqref{eq:ceiling} gives the paper's
decomposition, three channels and a constant:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:three}
h(p_t) \;=\; \tfrac{N}{2}\log(2\pi e) \;+\; \underbrace{H^{vol}_t}_{\text{scale}} \;+\; \underbrace{H^{dep}_t}_{\text{dependence}} \;-\; \underbrace{\DD_t}_{\text{shape}}.
\end{equation}

The shape channel is skewness and fat tails, and the Edgeworth expansion says
so explicitly: for a standardised variable with skewness $S$ and excess
kurtosis $K$ near Gaussianity,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:edgeworth}
J \;\approx\; \frac{S^2}{12} + \frac{K^2}{48}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:edgeworth} answers the question ``where would an entropy
jump be visible?'': in the third and fourth moments, additively, with a
skewness channel and a tail channel. It is also the source of the sign problem
that Section~\ref{sec:dynamics} must solve, since $S$ enters squared ---
\emph{negentropy cannot tell a crash from a rally}. As an estimator,
\eqref{eq:edgeworth} is unusable on daily returns; Section~\ref{sec:estimators}
documents how badly and what we use instead.

\subsection{The structural index}

The scale channel is a nuisance for our purpose: it dominates the level of
\eqref{eq:three} numerically, it moves with volatility, and a change of units
alters it. The other two channels are scale-free. Combining them, and using the
exact chain rule $\KL{p}{\prod_i q_i} = \sum_i \KL{p_i}{q_i} + I(x)$ where $I$
is the multi-information:

\begin{definition}[Structural information index]
\label{def:J}
\begin{equation}
\JJ_t \;\equiv\; \DD_t \;-\; H^{dep}_t \;=\; \KL{p_t}{\textstyle\prod_i q_{i,t}} \;\ge\; 0 ,
\end{equation}
the divergence of the conditional joint law of returns from a product of
Gaussian marginals with the same scales.
\end{definition}

$\JJ$ is zero if and only if returns are independent and Gaussian; it rises
when assets couple, when marginals fatten or when they skew. It is measured in
nats, it is invariant to the level of volatility, and it is additive over its
two channels, so that one can always ask how much of a move comes from
dependence and how much from shape. It is the state variable of
Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}.

\section{Information dynamics: one theorem, two postulates}
\label{sec:dynamics}

\subsection{Downward jumps are a theorem}

Two distinct statements underlie the downward half of the postulated
dynamics. Conflating them is an easy mistake, and earlier versions of this work
made it; we therefore separate them explicitly.

Recall first the definition of the \emph{mutual information} between the
return vector $X$ and a signal $Y$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mi}
I(X;Y) \;=\; \KL{p_{XY}}{p_X\otimes p_Y}
\;=\; \iint p(x,y)\,\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}\,dx\,dy ,
\end{equation}
that is, the divergence of the joint law from the product of the marginals: how
far the joint law is from independence. It is positive, symmetric, zero if and
only if $X\perp Y$, and --- unlike $h$ --- invariant under invertible
reparametrisation. For a scalar Gaussian pair with correlation $\rho$,
$I=-\tfrac12\log(1-\rho^2)$.

\begin{proposition}[Fall in average conditional entropy]
\label{prop:conditioning}
Let $X$ be the return vector and $Y$ a signal. Then
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{E}_Y\!\left[h(X\mid Y)\right] \;=\; h(X) - I(X;Y) \;\le\; h(X),
\end{equation*}
with equality if and only if $X\perp Y$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Monotonicity of the ceiling under constraint]
\label{prop:constraint}
Let $\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{C}'$ be two sets of constraints and
$\mathcal{Q}(\cdot)$ the set of laws satisfying them. Then
$\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{C}')\subseteq\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{C})$ and therefore
\begin{equation*}
\sup_{p\,\in\,\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{C}')} h(p) \;\le\; \sup_{p\,\in\,\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{C})} h(p).
\end{equation*}
\end{proposition}

Both results are classical \citep{cover2006} and they are not equivalent; the
detailed proof and a worked Gaussian example are in Appendix~\ref{app:mi}.
Their scopes differ along two axes.

Proposition~\ref{prop:conditioning} \emph{quantifies} the fall --- exactly
$I(X;Y)$ nats --- but only \emph{on average} over realisations of the signal.
For a particular $y$, $h(X\mid Y=y)$ can exceed $h(X)$: a sufficiently
confusing signal leaves the market more uncertain than before. One should
therefore expect a jump component that is mainly, not exclusively, one-sided
--- which is what Section~\ref{sec:results} finds.

Proposition~\ref{prop:constraint} holds on the contrary \emph{for every
realisation}, but concerns the maximum-entropy \emph{ceiling} rather than the
actual entropy, and it quantifies nothing: it says the ceiling cannot rise, not
by how much it falls.

What the two say together, and what motivates everything that follows: the
fall is \emph{discontinuous}, because the signal arrives at a point in time, and
its expected size in nats is the information content of the news --- an
amplitude measured on a scale that depends neither on the asset, nor on the
currency, nor on units.

\paragraph{A caveat in fairness.} Neither proposition is used to compute
anything in the empirical part. We measure $\JJ$, not
$h(X)-\mathbb{E}_Y[h(X\mid Y)]$: estimating a differential entropy in
dimension~20 on a 252-day window is out of reach (Appendix~\ref{app:units}).
The link between the theorem and the statistics reported below is an argument,
not an estimated identity, and the reader should take it as such.

\subsection{Two postulates}

\begin{postulate}[Relaxation]
\label{p:relax}
In the absence of new information, the structural index decays towards a
long-run level at a constant rate:
$d\JJ_t = -\kappa\left(\JJ_t-\bar\JJ\right)dt + \sigma_\JJ\,dW_t$ with $\kappa>0$.
\end{postulate}

The economics behind P\ref{p:relax} is that a constraint wears off. The news is
priced in, disagreement re-forms, positions disperse, and the cross-section
recovers the freedom the shock took away. Nothing in information theory
delivers this; it is the paper's substantive hypothesis, and $\kappa$ is the
object to estimate.

\paragraph{Why an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process and not something else?}
The choice is not one of convenience: it is \emph{forced} once three minimal
requirements are imposed, and it is the only one that satisfies all three.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Markov.} The future of $\JJ$ depends on the past only through its
present level. This is the parsimony requirement: postulate no memory beyond
what the current state already contains.
\item \emph{Stationary.} $\JJ$ has a finite long-run level and variance.
Without this the index drifts, it is not a state variable, and the regime
comparisons of Section~\ref{sec:results} are meaningless.
\item \emph{Gaussian noise.} Between two arrivals of information, we claim to
know nothing about the noise beyond its variance --- the maximum-entropy
principle applied to the noise itself, exactly as it was applied to returns in
Section~\ref{sec:bridge}.
\end{enumerate}

The theorem of \citet{doob1942} states that \textbf{the Ornstein--Uhlenbeck
process is the only process that is at once Markov, Gaussian and stationary} in
continuous time. The three requirements therefore leave no latitude for the
diffusive part: it is determined. The jump term, for its part, is added
precisely to \emph{break} Gaussianity where the framework says it fails, namely
at arrivals of information. The structure of \eqref{eq:ou} is thus an imposed
skeleton plus a deliberately non-Gaussian component, which is exactly the class
introduced by \citet{bns2001}.

\paragraph{What, on the other hand, remains arbitrary.} Three things, which
should be stated rather than left to look as if they followed from the theorem.
The rate $\kappa$ is assumed \emph{constant}: nothing forbids a crisis from
being absorbed faster, or slower, than ordinary news, and the data used here
cannot decide. The pull is \emph{linear} in the gap to the long-run level,
whereas an accelerated pull far from the mean would be just as plausible.
Finally, $\JJ$ is positive by construction whereas an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck with a
Brownian term can become negative: the specification of \citet{bns2001} avoids
this by dropping the Brownian term and driving the process with a
positive-jump subordinator, which guarantees a stationary law supported on
$\mathbb{R}^+$. Empirically the question does not arise --- $\JJ$ stays between
$2.17$ and $21.13$~nats on the main panel and between $0.42$ and $1.69$ on the
auxiliary European panel, very far from the boundary --- but formally one
should either drop $\sigma_\JJ$ or write the process on $\log\JJ$.

Finally, the postulate is not innocuous from a measurement standpoint: an
estimator with memory will manufacture decay even in the absence of any
economic relaxation, so Section~\ref{sec:placebo} measures the decay that a
memoryless world would already produce.

\begin{postulate}[Asymmetry of the constraint]
\label{p:sign}
The constraint that information imposes is one-sided in the direction of
losses, so that an entropy jump comes with negative skewness of the market
factor.
\end{postulate}

\paragraph{Why this postulate is logically necessary.} The index $\JJ$ is an
\emph{even} function of skewness: flipping the sign of skewness does not change
its value. The reason is immediate. The shape channel is
$\Dun(z)=\KL{p_z}{\varphi}$, and the normal law $\varphi$ is symmetric; so if
$z$ is replaced by $-z$, the reference law is unchanged and so is the
divergence:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:even}
\Dun(-z)=\Dun(z),\qquad\text{and therefore}\qquad \JJ(-x)=\JJ(x).
\end{equation}
The Edgeworth expansion \eqref{eq:edgeworth} makes this visible to the eye:
skewness $S$ enters squared.

The consequence is structural, not anecdotal. \textbf{A distribution with a
long left tail --- a crash --- and its mirror image with a long right tail --- a
rally --- have exactly the same $\JJ$.} The index measures the \emph{size} of
information, never its direction. No refinement of the estimator will change
this: it is a property of the object measured. If the paper wants to say
anything about direction, it must therefore add an outside ingredient.
P\ref{p:sign} is that ingredient, and it is the smallest possible one: a single
claim, about the sign, added to a framework that by construction produces none.

\paragraph{What it rests on.} Not a mechanism we would assert, but an
established empirical regularity: the \emph{leverage effect} in the sense of
\citet{black1976}, namely that negative returns are followed by larger
volatility increases than positive returns of the same size. It is one of the
most robust stylised facts of empirical finance
\citep{christie1982,engle1993,nelson1991}. In the language of this paper: for
the same amount of information, the market's response is larger on the
downside than on the upside, so a jump in $\JJ$ arrives together with negative
market skewness.

We deliberately abstain from choosing between competing causal explanations of
that effect --- financial leverage proper, volatility feedback, or asymmetric
withdrawal of liquidity on the sell side. The postulate depends on none of
them: it asserts only the asymmetry, not its cause. It is testable separately
(H5) and refutable separately, and Section~\ref{sec:results} indeed concludes
that it is not established.

\subsection{The resulting dynamics}

Proposition~\ref{prop:conditioning} together with P\ref{p:relax} gives a
non-Gaussian Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process with one-sided jumps, of the class
introduced by \citet{bns2001} for stochastic volatility:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ou}
d\JJ_t \;=\; -\kappa\left(\JJ_t-\bar\JJ\right)dt \;+\; \sigma_\JJ\,dW_t \;+\; \int_{z>0} z\,\mathcal{P}(dt,dz),
\end{equation}
The integral term needs careful reading, as its notation is compact.

\paragraph{What $\mathcal{P}$ is not.} It is neither a Gaussian law nor a
function. The letter $\mathcal N$ is reserved in this paper for the normal law,
so the jump measure carries a distinct symbol.

\paragraph{What $\mathcal{P}$ is.} A \emph{random counting measure}, known as a
Poisson random measure. One can picture it as a random scatter of points in the
$(\text{date},\ \text{size})$ plane: each point is an information event, its
abscissa the date at which it occurs and its ordinate the size of the jump it
causes. Formally, $\mathcal{P}(dt,dz)$ equals $1$ if there is a jump in the
time interval $[t,t+dt)$ whose size falls in $[z,z+dz)$, and $0$ otherwise. The
integral $\int_{z>0} z\,\mathcal{P}(dt,dz)$ then reads simply: ``if there is a
jump at date $t$, add its size $z$ to $\JJ$; otherwise add nothing''. It is a
compact way of writing $\sum_{j} z_j$, the sum of the jumps that occurred.

\paragraph{What characterises it.} Two ingredients, combined in its
\emph{intensity measure} $\nu(dz)\,dt$, the expected number of jumps of size
between $z$ and $z+dz$ during $dt$: an \emph{arrival rate}
$\lambda=\nu(\mathbb{R}^+)$, in jumps per unit time, and a \emph{size law}
$\nu(dz)/\lambda$. On the main panel, Section~\ref{sec:results} estimates
$\lambda\approx5$ jumps a year, a mean size of $0.62$~nat and a maximum of
$3.21$~nats.

\paragraph{What $z$ is.} The size of an individual jump, in nats.
Conceptually, it is the mutual information of the news that caused it, in the
sense of Proposition~\ref{prop:conditioning}. Operationally, it is the change in
$\JJ$ picked up by the four-sigma filter of Section~\ref{sec:estimators}. The
two coincide only approximately, and the paper does not claim to identify them.

\paragraph{Why the integral runs only over $z>0$.} Because $\JJ$ measures an
entropy \emph{deficit} and, by Proposition~\ref{prop:conditioning}, information
can only increase that deficit in expectation. In this framework nothing
creates a \emph{sudden increase} in entropy: entropy is restored only by the
slow relaxation of the first term, never by a jump. Hence one-sided jumps.

Two caveats, however. Proposition~\ref{prop:conditioning} is a statement
\emph{on average}: a particularly confusing realisation can increase entropy,
so a faithful specification would allow a small probability of negative jumps
rather than forbid them. And the Poisson measure assumes independent arrivals,
whereas the event study of Section~\ref{sec:results} shows clear clustering of
jumps; a self-exciting Hawkes-type process would be more faithful.

Note finally that the diffusion coefficient is written $\sigma_\JJ$ and not
$\eta$, the latter letter being reserved for the radius of stress scenarios
(Section~\ref{sec:stress}). Read on entropy itself rather than on $\JJ$, the
sign flips: entropy falls by jumps and recovers by diffusion. The observable
predictions are sharp, and they are what Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses} tests:
the law of $\Delta\JJ$ is right-skewed; large moves are up and small moves
down; and after an upward jump the index decays with half-life $\ln 2/\kappa$.

\subsection{Why fat tails, correlation and jumps arrive together}

The framework yields a prediction that no volatility or correlation model
yields on its own. It follows in four steps, without technical vocabulary.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Important news concerns \emph{all} assets at once: the same information
constrains each of them.
\item If the news is bad, all assets therefore fall \emph{on the same day}.
\item That day also appears, taken in isolation, in the history of each asset:
for it, it is an unusually large loss.
\item Accumulating such days deforms each asset's return distribution: the
loss side lengthens and thickens more than the gain side.
\end{enumerate}

Step 2 is what the dependence channel measures: assets move together, measured
correlation rises, $-H^{dep}$ rises. Step 4 is what the shape channel measures:
more and larger extreme losses than extreme gains, that is, higher kurtosis and
negative skewness, so $\DD$ rises. The two channels rise together because they
describe the same day seen from two places --- the cross-section for one, each
asset's distribution for the other.

In calm markets, notable moves are specific to each asset: they do not align
the cross-section and so have no reason to raise both channels at the same time.

The testable form is a \emph{regime-dependent coupling}: the correlation
between $-\Delta H^{dep}$ and $\Delta\DD$ should be close to zero in calm markets
and strongly positive under stress. That is H6, and it is where the paper
answers the question ``does the link between fat tails, correlation and entropy
jumps require an extra postulate?'': no. Given
Proposition~\ref{prop:conditioning}, the coupling follows from the common nature
of the news. Only the \emph{sign} requires P\ref{p:sign}.

\section{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

\begin{description}
\item[H1 (regime signature).] In the stress regime, the dependence channel
falls ($H^{dep}\downarrow$), the shape channel rises ($\DD\uparrow$) and hence
the structural index rises ($\JJ\uparrow$).
\item[H2 (partial offset).] Changes in the two channels of the Gaussian ceiling
are \emph{opposite in sign and proportional}: there is a coefficient $\beta$
such that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:h2}
\Delta H^{dep}_t \;=\; -\beta\,\Delta H^{vol}_t \;+\; u_t ,
\qquad \mathbb{E}[u_t]=0 ,
\end{equation}
and the hypothesis concerns the \emph{sign}: $\beta>0$. The coefficient $\beta$
is the \emph{offset rate}: the fraction of a scale-entropy move absorbed by
dependence. It reads directly --- $\beta=0$ means no offset, $\beta=1$ full
offset and hence conservation of $\det\Sigma$, $0<\beta<1$ partial offset,
$\beta<0$ amplification. A secondary hypothesis concerns regimes:
$\beta_{\text{stress}}>\beta_{\text{calm}}$.

This formulation replaces that of earlier versions of this project, which
asserted in one block that ``$H^{cov}$ is more stable than its components''.
That statement mixed two distinct claims --- a sign and a magnitude --- and could
only be tested in a binary way. Separating them makes it possible to
\emph{report} a coefficient with its confidence interval rather than pronounce a
verdict, and makes the comparison across datasets quantitative rather than
contradictory.
\item[H3 (jump asymmetry).] Changes in $\JJ$ are one-sided: at a common
threshold, upward jumps are more numerous and heavier than downward jumps, and
$\Delta\JJ$ is right-skewed --- \emph{beyond what the same estimator produces on
data with the same marginal law and no temporal structure}. The qualifier is
the whole hypothesis; without it the statement is untestable.
\item[H4 (relaxation).] $\JJ$ mean-reverts with $\kappa>0$ and a half-life
appreciably longer than the memory of the estimator itself, judged against the
same null.
\item[H5 (skewness signature).] Upward jumps in $\JJ$ coincide with negative
market skewness and with a rise in the skewness channel
(postulate P\ref{p:sign}).
\item[H6 (channel coupling).] The dependence and shape channels are weakly
coupled in calm markets and strongly coupled under stress.
\item[H7 (predictive power).] Knowing today's entropy says something about the
risk of the next 21~days that today's volatility alone does not already say.
``Improves'' is meant exclusively on dates the model had never seen at
estimation time; Section~\ref{sec:h7} spells out the protocol.
\item[H8 (composition of the crisis budget, exploratory).] Grouped into
episodes, jumps in $\JJ$ have the \emph{dependence} channel as their dominant
contributor more often than a resampling without temporal structure produces.
H8 is judged against the i.i.d.\ null only and serves only to calibrate the
composition of a scenario; it is treated in Appendix~\ref{app:explo}.
\end{description}

\section{Data and estimation}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Datasets}

The main panel gathers twenty S\&P~500 constituents at daily frequency, from
3~January 1990 to 28~December 2022 (8{,}312 return days; 8{,}061 rolling dates
after a 252-day window), from the \texttt{sp500} dataset distributed with the
\texttt{skfolio} package. These are \emph{individual stocks}, not industry
portfolios. The choice is deliberate: aggregating firms into industries
mechanically imposes common-factor dependence, and an earlier version of this
project cited a single-stock analysis as the robustness check it most needed.
The period covers the Asian and LTCM episodes of 1997--98, the technology
crash, the global financial crisis, the euro-area debt crisis, 2015--16, 2018,
COVID-19 and the 2022 correction.

The second panel is Kenneth French's daily 49-industry file
(\texttt{config/\allowbreak{}default.yaml}; the residual category is excluded, hence $48$
portfolios), 1990--2026, about $9{,}000$ dates. The full pipeline was run on
it, including both nulls at two hundred replications; the tables are in
\texttt{paper/\allowbreak{}tables/\allowbreak{}ff49\_\allowbreak{}*}. Contrasting individual stocks with industry
portfolios is the useful contrast: a signal that holds on both is not merely an
aggregation effect. Only these two panels carry verdicts against the nulls.

An auxiliary panel, \texttt{EuStockMarkets} \citep{bollerslev1996}, serves as
illustration: daily closing levels of the DAX, SMI, CAC and FTSE, 1{,}860
trading days from 1991 to 1998, $N=4$. It is far too small to carry a result,
has not been run against the two-hundred-replication nulls, and after the
burn-in period of the stress threshold only $1{,}104$ dates remain, $160$ of
them in stress. We report its numbers when they shed light on a point, never as
a replication.

\subsection{Estimators}
\label{sec:estimators}

\paragraph{Covariance.} Two estimators run in parallel. The reference is a
252-day rolling window with Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage \citep{ledoit2004}, which
reproduces the level statistics of the earlier version. The responsive
estimator, used for everything to do with jumps, is exponentially weighted with
a 60-day half-life plus $10\%$ shrinkage towards the diagonal. The choice
matters: with a rectangular window, an extreme observation \emph{leaving} the
sample produces a discontinuity in the entropy series on a date when nothing
happened, which would contaminate any jump test. Exponential weights remove
this artefact by construction.

\paragraph{Negentropy.} The shape channel is estimated marginally, asset by
asset, with the bounded-contrast approximation of \citet{hyvarinen1998}, whose
two terms split naturally into an odd (skewness) channel and an even (tail
weight) channel. The Edgeworth form \eqref{eq:edgeworth} is kept for
interpretation only, because it fails badly as an estimator exactly where
financial data live. Table~\ref{tab:negentropy} makes that failure explicit
against a nonparametric reference.

Two limitations deserve to be stated rather than buried. Estimating marginal
deficits and then adding the linear dependence term captures marginal
non-Gaussianity and Gaussian-copula dependence, but omits nonlinear tail
dependence; we measure it separately through an average pairwise
co-exceedance rate. And the Hyvärinen approximation, accurate in the
leptokurtic region, understates the negentropy of a moderately fat $t(8)$ by
about half, so the levels reported below are conservative.

\paragraph{Jump detection.} A jump in $\JJ$ is flagged when $|\Delta \JJ_t|$
exceeds four times a rolling local scale built on bipower variation
\citep{bns2004}, which is consistent for the diffusive scale even in the
presence of jumps and is lagged by one day so that the threshold judging a date
never contains it.

\paragraph{Regimes without look-ahead.} The stress regime is the top decile of
rolling 21-day market volatility. The threshold at date $t$ is the $90\%$
quantile of that volatility over dates strictly \emph{before} $t$ (expanding
quantile), and the first $504$ dates, lacking history, belong to no regime. An
earlier version of this project used the full-sample quantile, which uses future
volatility to label the past and inflates regime gaps
(Section~\ref{sec:verdicts}). The expanding threshold classifies more dates as
stress ($1{,}076$ out of $7{,}557$ instead of $807$) because market volatility
tends to rise over the sample: ``stress'' means ``more turbulent than
$90\%$ of everything seen so far'', which is more honest and changes the
definition. The same rule applies to the data and to the nulls.

\paragraph{Inference.} A 252-day rolling window means two consecutive
observations share 251 of their 252 underlying return days. Every regime
comparison therefore uses a 252-day circular block bootstrap, under a null that
breaks the value--label link while preserving the persistence of the series
and the clustering of the regime indicator. Predictive comparisons use
expanding-window walk-forward estimation refitted annually, evaluation on
non-overlapping dates, and a Diebold--Mariano test \citep{dm1995} on the
squared-error differential. Three conventions of this protocol were corrected
relative to the earlier version: the $p$-value of the relaxation slope is that
of the Dickey--Fuller (MacKinnon) distribution, because under a unit root the
normal law over-rejects; HAC lags follow the Newey--West rule
($\lfloor 4(n/100)^{2/9}\rfloor$, i.e.\ $10$ here) except for forecast targets
that overlap over 21~days (H7), where they are at least $21$; and the day after
a jump is no longer excluded from the relaxation estimate, because excluding it
selected on the dependent variable; the measured effect is negligible ($120.1$
then $120.8$~days on the main panel).

\subsection{The placebo null}
\label{sec:placebo}

The structural index is a positive convex functional of estimated second, third
and fourth moments. Estimation noise alone pushes it \emph{up} sharply and lets
it decay \emph{smoothly} --- which is exactly the signature that \eqref{eq:ou}
predicts. A jump-asymmetry statistic or a relaxation half-life is therefore
evidence only relative to this mechanical null, never relative to zero.

We measure this null in two ways. First a controlled simulation
(\texttt{scripts/\allowbreak{}estimator\_\allowbreak{}memory.py}): i.i.d.\ Gaussian returns with constant
covariance --- a world without any dynamics --- passed through the production
pipeline. A single injected stress day produces a 2.13-nat spike in $\JJ$ that
decays with a half-life of \textbf{35 trading days}, purely through estimator
memory; on clean data without any event, the four-sigma detector flags
\textbf{37 upward jumps and zero downward jumps} and $\Delta\JJ$ has a skewness
of $+2.9$. The mechanical asymmetry is real and it is large.

Second, two calibrated nulls (\texttt{scripts/\allowbreak{}placebo\_\allowbreak{}null.py}) that resample
the rows of the observed return matrix, preserving the cross-sectional
dependence and fat marginal tails of the real data. The i.i.d.\ version
destroys everything the postulates are about --- volatility clustering,
time-varying dependence, information-arrival dynamics. The block version
resamples 21-day blocks, so that short-term clustering survives and only
longer-horizon regime dynamics are destroyed. Two hundred replications of each
give the null distributions reported in Table~\ref{tab:placebo}. Both apply the
identical volatility filter to define stress regimes, so that the regime
comparisons of H1, H2 and H6 are tested on the same footing as they are
estimated.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{What the calibrated null leaves standing}
\label{sec:verdicts}

Table~\ref{tab:placebo} is the paper's central empirical result and it is
uncomfortable. Two nulls are built, both by resampling the observed return
matrix and running the identical pipeline --- including the volatility filter
that defines the stress regime --- two hundred times each, on each of the two
panels.

\begin{description}
\item[The i.i.d.\ null] resamples rows independently. It keeps fat marginal
tails and cross-sectional dependence and destroys \emph{all} temporal
structure. It asks: beyond the fact that turbulent windows contain large
returns, does the measure detect anything?
\item[The block null] resamples circular 21-day blocks. It additionally keeps
short-term volatility clustering and destroys only longer-horizon regime
dynamics. It asks the harder question: beyond clustering, does the measure
detect information dynamics?
\end{description}

Neither null is nested in the other in a way that would make one uniformly
conservative, and they disagree often enough that reporting only one would be
misleading. A statistic that clears both is evidence; a statistic that clears
one and fails the other is not identified.

\paragraph{What clears both nulls.} Two things, and only two. The regime gap of
the \emph{dependence} channel: $-0.99$~nat against $-0.21$ (i.i.d., none of the
two hundred replications reaches it) and $-0.51$ (block, $p=0.030$: six

index: $18.95$~nats against $10.05$ (i.i.d., no replication reaches it) and

whose $95\%$ quantile is $17.88$). In other words: what separates a crisis from
a mere run of large returns is that the cross-section couples, and the
structural index travels further in the real market than in any resampling of
the same returns.

\paragraph{What correcting the stress threshold removed.} In an earlier version
of this project, the gap in $\JJ$ cleared the i.i.d.\ null ($2.89$ against
$2.18$, $p=0.02$). With a stress threshold computed on the past only, the gap
falls to $+1.93$~nat, less than the mean of both nulls ($2.14$ and $1.97$,
$p=0.67$ and $0.50$): it no longer clears either. The dependence-channel gap
has also shrunk ($-1.72$ before, $-0.99$ now), but consistently in the data
and in the nulls, and it still clears both.

\paragraph{What does not clear.} Jump asymmetry fails the i.i.d.\ null outright
(H3). Channel coupling fails both, and fails in the direction that matters: the
i.i.d.\ null couples \emph{more} strongly than the data ($p=0.01$), and so does
the block null on average, without the difference being clear-cut (H6). The
regime signal of the shape channel ($+0.27$~nat) is smaller than that of the
i.i.d.\ null ($+1.25$) and, without the difference being clear-cut, than that of
the block null ($+0.65$, $p=0.08$). The offset hypothesis is lost in the nulls'
own noise (H2).

\paragraph{What is not identified.} The relaxation half-life is $121$~days
against $92$ under the i.i.d.\ null and $163$ under the block null --- the data
sit \emph{between} the two, and the sign of the comparison flips (only $8.5\%$
of block replications fall below $121$~days, against no i.i.d.\ replication
above). Block resampling sometimes concatenates high-volatility blocks and
manufactures long excursions; i.i.d.\ resampling manufactures short ones. The
honest conclusion is that the level of the half-life is an artefact of which
null one chooses to believe, and that P\ref{p:relax} is supported by the
amplitude result rather than by the half-life. The skewness signature (H5) has
the right sign against both nulls but does not pass the $5\%$ threshold
($p=0.10$ and $0.08$ for the correlation between $\Delta\JJ$ and
$\Delta S_{mkt}$), which is suggestive and no more.

\paragraph{A remark on $\JJ$ and volatility.} Under the block null, the
correlation between $\JJ$ and log-volatility is $0.26$; in the data it is $0.46$
($p<0.005$). The structural index tracks volatility \emph{more} closely than a
clustering-only process, not less. Table~\ref{tab:vol} shows that the shape
channel nevertheless stays orthogonal to volatility: the extra tracking
therefore goes through the dependence channel --- correlation and volatility
genuinely move together in a way that clustering alone does not reproduce.

\paragraph{The same test on industry portfolios.} Table~\ref{tab:placebo-ff49}
reproduces exactly the pipeline and both nulls (two hundred replications each)
on Kenneth French's 48 daily industry portfolios, 1990--2026, with the same
look-ahead-free stress threshold. This is the first time this panel is
confronted with the nulls. The regime gap of the \emph{dependence} channel
clears both: $-3.65$~nats against $-0.56$ (i.i.d., no replication) and $-2.46$
(block, $p=0.025$), about six times and one and a half times the null. The
magnitude relative to the nulls is similar on single stocks and on industries
($4.7$ and $1.9$ times the null), which argues against an industry-aggregation
artefact. The gap in $\JJ$, on the other hand, no longer clears anything
($+5.93$ against $7.95$ and $7.79$; it cleared both before the threshold
correction) and the rise of the deficit $\DD$ under stress is below that of
both nulls ($0.98$ against $4.81$ and $2.60$). The \emph{range} of $\JJ$ clears
only the i.i.d.\ null ($54.5$ against $36.9$) and not the block null ($45.9$,
$p=0.13$): the second result of the main panel does not replicate here. The
half-life ($190$~days) again sits between the two nulls ($101$ and $199$), the
count of upward jumps ($182$) is that of both nulls ($182$ and $176$), and
channel coupling under stress stays below that of the i.i.d.\ null. A remark
for H2: the correlation of $-0.63$ between $\Delta H^{vol}$ and $\Delta H^{dep}$,
which the earlier version of the paper presented as the industry offset, is
approached by the block null ($-0.54$, $p=0.09$); it clears only the i.i.d.\
null ($-0.36$).

Every sign is the one H1 predicts. Dependence entropy falls by 0.99~nat ($1.66$
with the EWMA estimator), the effective number of independent risk modes goes
from 13.1 to 11.4 out of 20, the shape deficit rises by 0.27~nat (not
significant, $p=0.16$), and the structural index rises by 1.94~nat --- a $38\%$
increase, the most significant difference in the table. The auxiliary panel
agrees on the signs of dependence and of $\JJ$ (from 0.92 to 1.15~nat,
$p=0.034$) but its sample, cut by the burn-in period to $1{,}104$ dates of which
$160$ are in stress, no longer carries a significant dependence difference
($-0.19$, $p=0.17$).

Against the calibrated null, the hypothesis splits in two. The
\emph{dependence} channel carries a real signal: the observed regime gap of
$-0.99$~nat is about five times the $-0.21$ of the i.i.d.\ null and twice the
$-0.51$ of the block null; it lies outside all two hundred replications of the
first and all but six of the second. The gap in $\JJ$ itself, which cleared the
i.i.d.\ null before the threshold correction, no longer clears either null
($1.93$ against $2.14$ and $1.97$): it is the dependence component that carries
H1, not the aggregate index. The \emph{shape} channel does not: its observed
regime gap of $+0.27$~nat is below the $+1.25$ of the i.i.d.\ null (and the
$+0.65$ of the block null). Shuffled data, in which a ``stress'' window is by
construction a window containing isolated extreme returns, fatten the measured
marginals more than real stress episodes do. The rise of $\DD$ under stress is
therefore evidence of nothing beyond the presence of large returns.

The economically meaningful part of H1 is thus narrower than the raw table
suggests, and it is the part one would want: what distinguishes a real crisis
from a run of large returns is that \emph{the cross-section couples} --- the
effective number of independent risk factors collapses --- not that the
marginals fatten.

\paragraph{The \citet{forbes2002} bias.}\label{sec:forbes} The stress regime is
defined by market volatility, and a correlation measured on a high-volatility
subsample is mechanically higher, even when the underlying correlation does not
change. Part of the raw gap of Table~\ref{tab:h1} is therefore this selection
bias. Both nulls contain it by construction: they resample whole rows of the
return matrix, hence a joint law of same-day returns that does not vary over
time, and they recompute the regime with the same volatility filter. In a
resampled world, the dependence-channel gap between regimes is \emph{only} the
selection effect, and that is what the $-0.21$ and $-0.51$ of
Table~\ref{tab:placebo} measure. The observed excess is what remains beyond it.
The nulls do not cover everything, and we say so: neither is a process with
persistent volatility and constant conditional correlation (CCC-GARCH type),
which would be the sharpest null for this question; the block null is only an
approximation to it, and it is against the block null that the result is most
fragile ($p=0.030$). Nor have we applied the analytical correction of
\citet{forbes2002}, which assumes a one-factor model.

Two entries of the table are as instructive as the confirmations. The
\emph{level} of market skewness barely moves between regimes ($p=0.81$): stress
is not a state in which returns are persistently more left-skewed, a point we
return to with H5, where the skewness signature appears at the time of the
jump rather than throughout the regime. And the Gaussian ceiling $H^{cov}$
\emph{rises} under stress, by 4.97~nats --- because the scale channel dominates
it. Differential entropy computed from a covariance matrix \emph{increases} in a
crisis. Any attempt to use the level of Gaussian entropy as a stress indicator
therefore has the sign backwards; that is precisely why the paper works with the
scale-free $\JJ$.

\subsection{H2: the offset is real, partial, and confined to stress}

An earlier version of this project, on the 49 Fama--French industry portfolios,
found a correlation of $-0.63$ between $\Delta H^{vol}$ and $\Delta H^{dep}$ over 1990--2026 and
a $57\%$ reduction in the variance of $\Delta H^{cov}$: marginal volatility and
dependence offset each other to the point of leaving the covariance
determinant markedly more stable than either component. Under the binary
formulation of the time, this result did not replicate on individual stocks and
the matter stopped there. Estimating \eqref{eq:h2} says something more precise.

\emph{The magnitude.} It is modest. A $\beta$ of $0.2$ to $0.4$ means that only

dependence; the rest passes into the ceiling. This is far from the $\beta=1$ that
a genuine ``conservation of covariance volume'' would require. The old
formulation, by speaking of ceiling stability, implicitly suggested $\beta$
close to $1$: that was too strong even where the phenomenon exists.

\emph{The fit.} The $R^2$ of \eqref{eq:h2} goes from $0.02$ to $0.16$ on the

stress. The postulated linear relation is not only of the right sign under
stress, it is also much better defined there.

\emph{Industry portfolios.} On French's $48$ industries, $\beta$ is
significantly positive in all three regimes, and \emph{larger in calm markets}
($+0.51$) than under stress ($+0.23$): the offset there is a permanent property
of industry aggregation rather than a crisis phenomenon, consistent with the
idea that aggregating firms into industries imposes common-factor dependence.
It clears only the i.i.d.\ null (stress $\beta$ of $+0.23$ against $0.08$) and
not the block null ($0.28$).

\paragraph{A caveat, and a question settled.} HAC standard errors remain
optimistic on rolling windows that share 251 of their 252 days, and the
Newey--West rule (10 lags here) is more so than the fixed 21 lags of the
earlier version; the intervals of Table~\ref{tab:h2} should be read as
indicative. As to whether $\beta_{\text{stress}}$ survives the calibrated null of
Section~\ref{sec:verdicts}, the question is now settled, and in the direction
this paragraph feared: \textbf{no}. Table~\ref{tab:placebo} now includes this
test. Under the i.i.d.\ null, the observed $\beta_{\text{stress}}$ of $0.20$ falls
inside the null distribution ($0.05$, interval $-0.33$--$0.31$, $p=0.40$);
under the block null, the same verdict ($0.11$, interval $-0.28$--$0.32$,
$p=0.47$). The same mechanical reason that lets resampling reproduce H6 ---
turbulent windows containing large common days --- suffices to produce a
positive $\beta$ slope under stress, without any economic offset existing in the
resampled data. \textbf{H2 in the form \eqref{eq:h2} is therefore a measured
regularity and not an established result}: the sign and order of magnitude
reported above are real, but a resampling with no offset dynamics produces an
equally positive $\beta_{\text{stress}}$, for the same mechanical reason as H6.
The verdict thus joins the one already reached on the correlation.

\paragraph{What this does not change.} The consequence that motivated
Section~\ref{sec:channels} stands: with $\beta$ of order $0.2$ outside crises and
a Gaussian ceiling that rises with volatility, the information content of a
crisis cannot be read in $\det\Sigma$. It has to be sought elsewhere.

\subsection{H3: the jump component is one-sided}

Taken at face value, the evidence looks overwhelming. On the main panel, the
four-sigma detector finds 158 upward jumps against 9 downward jumps; upward
jumps carry $83.8\%$ of the variance of $\Delta\JJ$ against $0.3\%$ for downward
jumps; the skewness of $\Delta\JJ$ is $+12.3$. The auxiliary panel agrees (27
against 5, skewness $+6.7$). Figure~\ref{fig:index} is visibly a sawtooth, with
sharp rises in 1997--98, 2002, September 2008, 2011, 2015, 2018 and a 21-nat
peak in March 2020, each followed by a slow decay --- exactly the shape
\eqref{eq:ou} predicts.

\textbf{The asymmetry does not survive the i.i.d.\ null.} Under that null, the
detector finds \emph{on average 148 upward and 3 downward jumps}, with a
skewness of $\Delta\JJ$ of $+14.8$ --- above the observed $+12.3$ --- and an
upward-jump variance share of $90\%$, again above the observed value. Every
one-sidedness statistic of the data lies at the edge of or inside this null.

The block null is more lenient on one point and not on the others. Preserving
volatility clustering lowers the null's count of upward jumps to $133.2$, so the
observed $158$ lies well beyond it ($p=0.01$); but the skewness of $\Delta\JJ$
($13.0$) and the upward-jump variance share ($0.82$) still bracket the observed
values, and the mean upward jump remains larger in the null than in the data. A
count that clears one null while every measure of the \emph{shape} of the
asymmetry fails both is not a result. H3 is \textbf{not supported}.

The explanation is not subtle once stated. $\JJ$ is a positive convex
functional of estimated second, third and fourth moments. A single fat-tailed
observation entering an exponentially weighted estimator pushes it up sharply,
and the estimator's own memory then lets it decay smoothly. Sawtooth dynamics
are what a fat-tailed marginal law produces when passed through this
measurement apparatus, whether or not information arrives in bursts. The
visual appeal of Figure~\ref{fig:index} is, to that extent, an artefact.

Two things are worth saving. First, the null is demanding and probably too
demanding for the postulate as stated: because i.i.d.\ resampling preserves fat
tails, it preserves the \emph{bursts} of information and scrambles only their
chronology. What the test rejects is therefore not ``information arrives in
discrete lumps'' but the weaker and more useful claim that \emph{the observed
one-sidedness of $\Delta\JJ$ carries information about arrival dynamics beyond
what the marginal law already implies}. It does not.

Second, one comparison in Table~\ref{tab:placebo} goes the other way and is
instructive. The largest observed upward jump is $3.21$~nats against a mean of
$6.86$ under the i.i.d.\ null, and the mean upward jump $0.62$ against $1.12$
(i.i.d.) and $0.84$ (block): real entropy jumps are about \emph{half} the size
the shuffled world produces. The reason is that real extreme days arrive when
the market is already turbulent, so the local scale against which they are
measured is already high; in the shuffled world they spring from a calm
baseline and look all the more surprising. This is volatility clustering
showing up in the entropy measure --- evidence of temporal structure, obtained
from a statistic built to test something else entirely.

\subsection{H4: relaxation, against the estimator's own memory}

Estimating \eqref{eq:ou} on non-jump dates only --- so that the diffusive channel
is identified without contamination --- gives $\kappa = 0.0057$ per day
($t=-4.70$, HAC with 10~lags, $p=0.0001$ under the Dickey--Fuller distribution),
a half-life of \textbf{121 trading days} and a long-run level of 3.30~nats. The
auxiliary panel gives $\kappa=0.0056$, a half-life of 124~days, $p=0.047$ under
Dickey--Fuller (0.004 under the normal law, which was the value reported until
now and which over-rejects).

The half-life is where the two nulls diverge most sharply, and the divergence
is instructive. Under i.i.d.\ resampling, the same estimator returns $92.1$~days
on average, so the observed $120.8$ exceeds every one of the two hundred
replications. Under block resampling, it returns $162.8$~days on average
($90\%$ interval: $116$--$228$), so the observed value falls \emph{below} $91\%$
of replications. The data sit between the two nulls, and the sign of the
comparison depends entirely on which one is asked. Block resampling sometimes
concatenates high-volatility blocks and manufactures long excursions; i.i.d.\
resampling breaks the runs that real markets contain and manufactures short
ones. The level of the half-life is therefore \textbf{not identified}, and the
figure of $121$~days must not be read as an absorption time --- the controlled
Gaussian simulation of Section~\ref{sec:placebo}, in which a single shock decays
in $35$~days without any economics, sets the floor of what belongs to estimator
memory.

What survives is the \emph{amplitude}. The range of $\JJ$ over the sample is
$18.95$~nats against $10.05$ under the i.i.d.\ null (none of the two hundred
replications reaches it) and $13.77$ under the block null ($p=0.035$; $95\%$
quantile of the null: $17.88$). Its standard deviation clears the i.i.d.\ null
but not the block null. Real markets move the structural index further than any
resampling of the same returns does, which is the part of P\ref{p:relax} the
data support: not a particular relaxation rate, but a state variable that
genuinely travels.

Figure~\ref{fig:event} adds a caveat that the point estimate hides. Averaging the
index around detected upward jumps shows that $\JJ$ starts rising some twenty
days \emph{before} the flagged date, peaks about thirteen days after, and
returns to its pre-event level after roughly eighty days. Information events
cluster: a flagged jump is typically not an isolated arrival but one element of
a sequence. The clean picture of a single jump followed by decay given by
\eqref{eq:ou} is therefore an idealisation, and a self-exciting Hawkes-type
arrival process would fit the observed clustering better than the Poisson
arrivals assumed here.

\subsection{H5: jumps carry a sign}

Postulate P\ref{p:sign} predicts that upward jumps come with negative market
skewness. On upward-jump dates, the change in market skewness averages $-0.052$
against $+0.001$ elsewhere; the level of market skewness on those dates is
$-0.394$ against $-0.214$; and $\mathrm{corr}(\Delta \JJ, \Delta S_{mkt}) =
-0.24$. The skewness channel of the deficit rises on jump dates ($+0.010$ against
$-0.0002$), as does tail co-exceedance ($+0.008$ against $-0.0002$, correlation
with $\Delta\JJ$ of $+0.35$). The auxiliary European panel is stronger on this
dimension: $\mathrm{corr}(\Delta\JJ,\Delta S_{mkt}) = -0.38$ and a mean skewness
change of $-0.129$ on upward-jump dates.

The caveat already visible in Table~\ref{tab:h1} is that the skewness signature
is an \emph{event} property, not a regime property: sorting dates by volatility
reveals no significant skewness difference, sorting them by entropy jump does.

Against the nulls, H5 is suggestive and nothing more. Both produce the effect
in the same direction without any economics, because the mechanical link is
obvious once stated: a large negative return simultaneously raises $\JJ$ and
lowers the measured skewness of the window it enters. The observed correlation
of $-0.243$ lies between the eighth and tenth percentile of both nulls
($-0.035$ i.i.d., $-0.069$ block; $p=0.10$ and $0.08$), and the mean skewness
change on jump dates ($-0.053$) stays inside both ($-0.011$ and $-0.027$;
$p=0.18$ and $0.22$). We report H5 as \emph{not established}: the direction is
right, it is consistent across two nulls and two datasets (the auxiliary panel
gives $-0.38$), but a $p$-value of $0.08$ to $0.10$ over two hundred
replications, hence above the $5\%$ threshold, is a reason to look further, not
a result. Postulate P\ref{p:sign} remains a postulate.

In the data, the pattern is striking. In calm markets, the dependence channel

Both nulls reproduce it too, and the i.i.d.\ null reproduces it \emph{more

measured correlation and fatter measured marginals, so the two channels move
together in any data where large days exist, shuffled or not. H6 is \textbf{not
supported} as evidence of a binding common constraint.

Adding the block null does change the interpretation of the gap between null and
data. Under i.i.d.\ resampling, the null's coupling is almost deterministic
($0.90$, standard deviation $0.03$); preserving clustering lowers it to $0.72$
with a standard deviation of $0.08$, closer to and now compatible with the
observed $0.63$. Real stress episodes couple the two channels \emph{less}
tightly than the i.i.d.\ null and no more than the block null. If the gap with
the i.i.d.\ null is not noise, it points away from Section~\ref{sec:dynamics}:
dependence and tail weight would have partly independent drivers in real
crises, which a common-constraint story does not predict.

Over the full sample, the variance of $\Delta\JJ$ splits $40\%$ dependence and
$60\%$ shape on individual stocks, and $69\%/31\%$ the other way round on the
four-index panel --- a reminder that the balance between channels depends on the
cross-section being measured.

\subsection{H7: entropy describes, it does not forecast}
\label{sec:h7}

\paragraph{The question, in one sentence.} If I know the market's entropy
today, do I know something about the risk of the next three weeks that today's
volatility did not already tell me?

\paragraph{The protocol, in four steps.} Two models forecasting the same thing
are compared --- realised volatility over the next 21~days, then drawdown, then
the worst daily loss. The \emph{baseline model} uses only the scale channel. The
\emph{augmented model} adds an entropy channel. Then:

\begin{enumerate}
\item both models are estimated on the past only, never on the future being
forecast;
\item a forecast is made and its error recorded;
\item each model is re-estimated every year with the elapsed year added, and the
process repeats;
\item errors are compared only on dates 21~days apart, so that two successive
forecasts never concern the same days.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What ``confirmed'' would mean here.} Only this: that the augmented
model makes, \emph{on dates it had not seen when estimated}, smaller errors than
the baseline, and that the gap is too large to be chance --- which the test of
\citet{dm1995} decides. The point deserves emphasis, because it is where most
indicators of this kind fool themselves: \textbf{adding any variable
mechanically improves the fit on the data used to estimate the model}. A good
in-sample fit proves nothing at all.

\paragraph{The verdict.} H7 is rejected. No channel --- not $\JJ$, nor the
deficit $\DD$, nor the dependence term, nor the crisis indicator, nor the
unabsorbed share --- significantly improves the out-of-sample forecast. The best
improvement observed, across all channels and targets, is $3.3\%$ of mean
squared error with $p=0.23$: indistinguishable from chance. Several
specifications are significantly \emph{worse} than the baseline.

And the exact illustration of the trap described above: \emph{in} sample, the
coefficients are highly significant, $p<10^{-4}$ for $\JJ$ in every
specification. This is the pattern one expects from overlapping windows, and it
is why these figures are not presented as evidence.

\paragraph{What to conclude.} That entropy channels are \emph{diagnostic}, not
\emph{predictive}: they describe the market's present informational state well,
they do not anticipate its next move under a linear specification. This result
replicates, on another dataset and under a markedly more demanding protocol,
the negative predictive result obtained by the earlier version of this project.
The value of the framework is therefore to be sought where
Section~\ref{sec:stress} seeks it --- in internal consistency and scenario
calibration --- and not in point forecasts.

\section{Entropy stress testing}
\label{sec:stress}

\subsection{The construction}

\begin{definition}[Entropy-ball scenario]
Given today's conditional distribution $p_0$ and a severity $\eta>0$, the
scenario of severity $\eta$ for a risk functional $\rho$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ball}
p^\star_\eta \;=\; \arg\max_{p\,:\,\KL{p}{p_0}\,\le\,\eta} \rho(p).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

For a linear portfolio and a Gaussian reference, only the one-dimensional
projection matters. With $m_0,s_0$ the portfolio mean and standard deviation and
$u=(m-m_0)/s_0$, $v=s/s_0$, the constraint is
$g(u,v)=\tfrac12\!\left(u^2+v^2-1-2\log v\right)\le\eta$, and for any $\rho$ of
the form $-m+cs$ --- Gaussian VaR and expected shortfall are both of that form
--- the solution satisfies $u=-t$, $v-v^{-1}=ct$, where $t$ is pinned down by
$g=\eta$. The special case $c=0$ is the sanity check to remember:

\begin{equation}
\text{a purely directional scenario of }\eta\text{ nats is exactly a move of }\sqrt{2\eta}\text{ sigmas.}
\end{equation}

One nat buys $1.41$~sigma. This is the exchange rate between information and
risk, and it is the whole reason the construction is usable by a committee: the
severity parameter is not an abstraction.

\subsection{Calibrating the radius}
\label{sec:calib}

The radius is not a modelling choice here. Define the $h$-step information flow
\begin{equation}
I^{(h)}_t \;=\; \KL{p_t}{p_{t-h}},
\end{equation}
the divergence between the market's conditional distribution today and $h$ days
ago --- how much the market has revised its own view. The divergence must,
however, be measured on the same object as the constraint. The
ball~\eqref{eq:ball} of the previous section constrains only the
\emph{portfolio projection}: the law of $w^\top r$, in one dimension. The radius
must therefore be read off the information flow of that same projection,
$I^{(h)}_t=\KL{p_t^{\,w}}{p_{t-h}^{\,w}}$, and not off that of the whole return
vector, which grows with the number of assets and overstates the projection. An
earlier version of this project calibrated the one-dimensional ball on the
$N$-dimensional flow: its radii were about ten times too large at the 10-year
rung ($3.88$~nats instead of $0.41$), which produced a 10-year volatility
multiplier of $3.2$ instead of $1.6$. Quantiles of $I^{(21)}$, thinned into
non-overlapping blocks, convert a return period into a radius. The thinning
depends on its starting phase (one date in 21 is kept): the reported radius is
the median of the quantiles over the $21$ possible phases, and their range is
given in Table~\ref{tab:ladder}.

validated measure of crisis frequency.

Table~\ref{tab:classical} is the applied point of the paper, and what it
establishes is narrower than was once written. What holds: for the same tail
loss, the textbook bundle costs more than it needs to. The standard bundle
delivers an expected shortfall of $13.7\%$; an entropy scenario delivers it for
$5.47$~nats, where the portfolio pays $7.24$~nats for the bundle, an
overpayment of $24\%$ ($33\%$ for the moderate bundle, $15\%$ for the severe;
$15$, $11$ and $6\%$ on French's industries). The reason is structural: the
bundle also deforms directions the portfolio does not see. What no longer holds:
the reading ``the committee thinks it specified a thirty-year event and bought
a ten-year one'', which compared a twenty-dimensional price to a projection
radius. On the consistent portfolio scale, all three bundles lie beyond the
largest radius observed in 33~years, and the limitation of the previous
paragraph forbids turning that into a return period: one can say they are
overpaid, not that they are thirty-year events.

Where does the money go? The directional component is cheap and exactly
priced: moving the portfolio mean by $k$ standard deviations costs $k^2/2$~nats,
so $4.5$ for three sigmas. The expensive component is the uniform doubling of
every marginal volatility, at $16.1$~nats on its own --- more than the whole
bundle. Forcing all correlations to $0.9$ costs $8.1$~nats on its own.

The components are not additive, and the way they fail to be is instructive:
the three sum to $28.7$~nats while the bundle costs $9.97$. Forcing correlations
up collapses the covariance onto a near-rank-one subspace to which the reference
distribution already grants generous variance --- the market mode --- so scaling
that collapsed structure is much cheaper than scaling the full-rank structure
($\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_0^{-1}\Sigma_1)$ drops from $20$ to $10.5$ when correlations
are forced, before the volatility multiplier is even applied). The volatility
shock and the correlation shock partly pay for each other.

This is the structure described by the dependence channel of H1, the only
regime result to clear both nulls. Real crises do not equalise correlations and
do not inflate every volatility independently; they load the cross-section onto
one or two collective modes. A framework that shocks volatility, correlation and
shape as three separate scalars has no way of representing this, nor any way of
noticing that its bundle buys severity with a $15$ to $33\%$ margin.

Four concrete advantages over the volatility/mean/skewness approach follow.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A single severity scale, in a unit that does not depend on the
asset.} Scenarios become comparable across desks, portfolios and asset
classes. Severity could be quoted with a return period rather than an
adjective, once the ladder is validated against history, which it is not here
(Section~\ref{sec:calib}).
\item \textbf{Internal consistency by construction.} Location and scale shocks
come from one budget and one optimisation. A bundle that shocks volatility,
correlation and skewness independently can describe, and often does describe,
a distribution the market could not produce.
\item \textbf{Existing scenarios can be audited rather than replaced.} Any
regulatory template or committee judgement receives a price in nats, so that
the question of whether a stress test is calibrated becomes arithmetic. This is
the practical benefit of Table~\ref{tab:classical}.
\item \textbf{The reverse stress test comes for free.} Instead of asking what a
scenario does, one asks what the cheapest scenario producing a given loss is.
On the main panel, the empirical $99\%$ daily loss ($3.2\%$) costs $1.5$~nats;
the $99.9\%$ loss ($7.7\%$) costs $4.8$; the worst day in 33~years ($11.5\%$)
costs $9.0$. These prices are for a one-day horizon and the empirical law of the
loss; they do not read directly on the 21-day ladder of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}.
\end{enumerate}

A caveat on the fourth point. Reverse stress tests computed nonparametrically,
by tilting the empirical sample, degenerate as the radius grows: at
$9.0$~nats, the tilted measure has an effective sample size of $1.0$, that is,
it is the worst single day dressed up as a distribution. The implementation
reports the effective sample size for exactly this reason, and the Gaussian-ball
construction should be used as soon as it drops below a few dozen.

\subsection{Two monitoring indicators}

The jump/diffusion split yields two quantities that can be produced daily at
negligible cost. The \emph{crisis} indicator is the standardised upward-jump
component of $\JJ$: how much information arrived today, in units of the local
diffusive scale, zero when no jump is detected. The \emph{unabsorbed share}
indicator is $\JJ_t$ relative to its rolling maximum: how much of what has
already arrived has not yet relaxed. Under P\ref{p:relax}, it should decay with
half-life $\ln2/\kappa$; a market where it stays close to one appreciably
longer is a market where the shock has not been absorbed. Neither improves a
volatility forecast (H7); both describe a state that volatility does not.

\subsection{Relevance for insurance solvency}

Three points connect this to the insurance strand of the wider project. First,
an entropy-ball scenario is a natural object for the ORSA: it produces a
complete stressed distribution, not a vector of shocks, so it feeds a capital
calculation directly and carries a plausibility statement that remains to be
validated (see the limitations). Second, the component decomposition of
Table~\ref{tab:classical} concerns the Solvency~II standard formula, whose
diversification credit is built on a fixed correlation matrix: shocking
volatilities and correlations as separate scalars, the usual conservative
adjustment, buys a given tail loss at a $15$ to $33\%$ information overpayment,
because it does not represent the way observed crises redistribute dependence
--- onto a few collective modes rather than uniformly. Finally, the coherence
properties of entropic risk measures \citep{ahmadijavid2012,artzner1999} make
the $\JJ$ framework compatible in principle with internal-model capital
requirements, although nothing here has been tested on liability portfolios
and the short, low-frequency panels typical of insurance data would strain every
estimator used above.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{The bridge is an argument, not a proof.}
Section~\ref{sec:bridge-caveats} lists the failures of linear impact and of
sign exchangeability. Nothing empirical below depends on the volume clock, but
the framing does.

\paragraph{The deficit is estimated marginally.} The shape channel captures
marginal non-Gaussianity and Gaussian-copula dependence; nonlinear tail
dependence is measured separately rather than integrated, so $\JJ$ understates
the true divergence, by an amount itself largest under stress.

\paragraph{The mechanical null swallows a whole hypothesis.} Jump asymmetry is
entirely reproduced by i.i.d.\ data with the same marginal law, and about two
thirds of the measured relaxation belongs to estimator memory. The amplitude
result survives; the half-life is not identified; the asymmetry result does not
survive, and no reader should quote the 158-to-9 figure without the null next
to it. The two hundred replications of Table~\ref{tab:placebo} tightened the
$p$-values, and they moved two verdicts: the range of $\JJ$ now clears the block
null only narrowly ($p=0.035$) and the skewness signature (H5) no longer passes
the $5\%$ threshold.

\paragraph{Poisson arrivals are the wrong model.} Figure~\ref{fig:event} shows
clustering that \eqref{eq:ou} does not accommodate. A self-exciting Hawkes
arrival process is the obvious next specification.

\paragraph{Two panels, of modest size.} The dependence channel is sensitive to
$N$, the balance between channels shifts with it, and the KL radius is not
comparable from one universe to another. Moving from twenty stocks to
forty-eight industry portfolios keeps the dependence-channel result but not the
one on the range of $\JJ$. The two panels do not cover the same period either
(1990--2022 and 1990--2026). A broad cross-section of individual stocks, CRSP
style, remains to be done before any figure presented here is treated as a
constant of nature.

\paragraph{No head-to-head comparison with the absorption ratio.} The dependence
channel belongs to the family of concentration measures of the correlation
matrix (Section~\ref{sec:related}). We have not confronted it, on the same data
and against the same nulls, with the absorption ratio of \citet{kritzman2011};
nothing here says it does better.

\paragraph{The severity ladder extrapolates in the tail, and looks too mild.}
With 383 non-overlapping observations of information flow, rungs beyond about
twenty years are interpolations of the empirical tail, not measurements.
Fitting a generalised Pareto tail to $I^{(h)}$ would be the disciplined
correction. More serious: once the radius is calibrated on the portfolio
projection, as the ball requires, the ladder gives a 10-year volatility
multiplier of $1.6$ against a 10-year quantile of realised volatility increase
of $3.2$ (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). The return periods attached to scenarios are
therefore not validated, and we do not quote them as frequencies; only the
relative efficiency (the bundles' overpayment) and the calibrated composition
withstand this defect.

\paragraph{A look-ahead-free stress threshold, but not a choice-free one.} The
expanding threshold excludes the first two years of each panel, classifies
more dates as stress than the full-sample quantile, and strips the gap in $\JJ$
of its status as a result. It also brings the European panel (four indices,
eight years) down to $1{,}104$ dates of which $160$ are in stress, where no
regime difference of the dependence channel is significant any more.
Newey--West HAC lags are shorter than the fixed $21$ lags used before, hence
more optimistic on rolling windows.

\paragraph{Nothing here forecasts.} H7 is rejected on two datasets under a
demanding protocol. Any use of these measures should be diagnostic.

\paragraph{The exploratory part clears only one null.} H8 is not identified
against the block null, at any block length tested between 5 and 126~days
(Table~\ref{tab:blocksens}). The calibrated composition that uses it
(Appendix~\ref{app:explo}) starts from a default uniform dilation that is not a
minimum of the ambient divergence, and its result is specific to this panel's
correlation structure. A reader who wanted to use H8 to distinguish causally
distinct ``types'' of crisis should treat it as not established.

\section{Conclusion}

The theoretical contribution is an explicit chain stating, at each link, what is
theorem and what is hypothesis. Maximum entropy under first- and second-moment
constraints gives a Gaussian; applied in volume time, with traded volume
supplying the variance budget, it gives Gaussian returns conditional on volume,
and temporal consistency turns that into a subordinated Brownian motion and then
a geometric Brownian price. Read backwards, non-Gaussian prices measure the
distance from maximal ignorance, and that distance splits into a dependence
channel and a shape channel whose sum, the structural index $\JJ$, is
scale-free and positive. Conditioning cannot increase expected entropy, so
entropy falls by jumps whose \emph{expected} size is the mutual information of
the news; that this entropy recovers by diffusion, and that the fall is
one-sided in the direction of losses, are the two postulates the paper adds and
tests.

The empirical picture is above all a warning, and we think that is the useful
result. Every descriptive claim of the framework is visible in the data: stress
raises the structural index (by $1.9$~nat), the index is a sawtooth, jumps are
one-sided, they carry negative skewness, and the dependence and tail channels
couple under stress. Passed through a null that keeps the marginal law and
scrambles the chronology, most of it disappears --- and several statistics come
out \emph{larger} in the shuffled data than in the real data. A positive convex
functional of estimated second, third and fourth moments jumps up and decays
smoothly as soon as a fat-tailed observation enters the estimator, whether or
not information arrives in bursts. This warning applies to any entropy-based
crisis indicator, not only to this one; to our knowledge, the applied literature
does not state it in this form.

Exactly two statistics clear both nulls on the main panel, and they are the two
to keep; on the FF49 industry portfolios only the regime gap of the dependence
channel clears both, so that it, and it alone, holds on both panels. What
separates a crisis from a run of large returns is that the cross-section
couples: the regime signal of the dependence channel is between one and a half
and six times that of the nulls, while that of the shape channel is below both.
And the structural index travels further over the sample than in any resampling
of the same returns. The relaxation rate itself is not identified --- it sits
between the two nulls --- so P1 is supported by amplitude and not by a
half-life. Two further results go against the paper's interest: the
volatility--dependence offset obtained on industry portfolios does not survive
the move to individual stocks, and no entropy channel forecasts anything out of
sample.

The applied contribution is what the framework offers once one stops asking it
to forecast. Measuring the severity of a scenario in nats gives stress testing a
unit; calibrating that unit on the market's realised information flow would
give it a frequency, were the resulting ladder not milder than history
(Section~\ref{sec:calib}); and solving for the worst distribution in the
resulting ball gives a scenario that cannot be internally inconsistent. Applied
in reverse, it prices the scenarios institutions already run: on this sample, a
standard textbook bundle buys its tail loss at a $24\%$ overpayment ($15$ to
$33\%$ depending on severity), because it spends most of its budget inflating
every marginal volatility uniformly --- which is not the path observed crises
take.

Appendix~\ref{app:explo} pushes this observation further, on an exploratory
basis: it composes a scenario whose dependence share is anchored on H1 and
refined by an eighth hypothesis that clears only the i.i.d.\ null. What remains
established fits in two sentences. Entropy-based crisis indicators must be
judged against a resampling of the same returns, or most of their signatures
are measurement artefacts. And what withstands that test, on individual stocks
and on industries alike, is the coupling of the cross-section under stress.

\section{Units, invariance, and what is computable}
\label{app:units}

\paragraph{The nat.} Entropy $H=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i$ is defined only once the
base of the logarithm is chosen, and that choice \emph{is} the choice of unit.
With $\log_2$ one gets bits (shannons), with $\log_{10}$ dits, and with the
natural logarithm \emph{nats}. One nat is the information delivered by an
observation that divides the space of possibilities by $e\approx2.718$, where a
bit divides it by two. The conversion is
$1~\text{nat}=1/\log 2\approx1.4427$~bit. This whole paper uses the natural
logarithm: entropies, deficits, divergences, scenario radii and the structural
index are in nats.

Three operational readings, from the most useful to the most abstract. First
the exchange rate established in Section~\ref{sec:stress}: a purely directional
scenario of $\eta$ nats is a move of $\sqrt{2\eta}$ sigmas, so $k$~sigmas cost
$k^2/2$ nats. Then coding: $\KL{p}{q}$ nats is the expected excess code length
when data with law $p$ are encoded with a code optimal for $q$. Finally
hypothesis testing: a divergence of one nat per observation means that the
likelihood ratio in favour of $p$ grows on average by a factor $e$ with each
observation.

\paragraph{$h$ depends on units, divergences do not.} The differential entropy
of a continuous variable satisfies, for any invertible affine transformation,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:h-scaling}
h(aX+b) \;=\; h(X) + \log|a| ,
\qquad\text{and in dimension } N,\quad h(AX+b)=h(X)+\log|\det A| .
\end{equation}
Expressing returns in percent rather than in decimals therefore adds
$N\log 100$ to $h$: the \emph{level} of $h$, and hence that of $H^{vol}$ and
$H^{cov}$, has no interpretable absolute value. By contrast $\KL{p}{q}$,
$I(X;Y)$, $\DD$ and $\JJ$ are invariant under any invertible change of
variable, the Jacobians cancelling between numerator and denominator of the
logarithm. \emph{A figure in nats is comparable across datasets only if it is a
divergence.} That is the structural reason why the paper builds a scale-free
index.

\paragraph{How is $h(X)$ computed?} In the Gaussian case, in closed form
(Appendix~\ref{app:gauss}). In general, it cannot be without knowing $p$.
Nonparametric estimators exist --- the spacings of \citet{vasicek1976},
Kozachenko--Leonenko nearest neighbours, kernel methods --- but all degrade
quickly beyond three or four dimensions. Estimating a differential entropy in
dimension~20 on a 252-day window is out of reach.

The paper therefore \emph{never} computes $h(p)$. It computes $h(q)$ in closed
form from $\widehat\Sigma$, and the deficit $\DD$ through an approximation of
the \emph{difference} of marginal entropies. A difference of entropies is
dimensionless and estimable; a bare differential entropy is not. The whole
architecture --- decompose rather than estimate directly --- follows from this
constraint.

\section{Gaussian entropy: proof of equation~\eqref{eq:ceiling}}
\label{app:gauss}

\begin{proof}[Step A: entropy of a multivariate Gaussian]
For $q=\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)$ on $\mathbb{R}^N$,
\[
\log q(x) = -\tfrac12\left[N\log 2\pi + \log\det\Sigma + (x-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right],
\]
so
\[
h(q) = -\mathbb{E}_q[\log q] = \tfrac12 N\log 2\pi + \tfrac12\log\det\Sigma
+ \tfrac12\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[(X-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(X-\mu)\right].
\]
The last expectation follows from the trace trick: a scalar equals its trace,
and the trace is cyclic, hence
\[
\mathbb{E}\!\left[(X-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(X-\mu)\right]
= \mathbb{E}\!\left[\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\Sigma^{-1}(X-\mu)(X-\mu)'\right)\right]
= \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma) = \operatorname{tr}(I_N) = N .
\]
Collecting terms, $h(q)=\tfrac12\left[N\log2\pi+\log\det\Sigma+N\right]
=\tfrac12\log\!\left((2\pi e)^N\det\Sigma\right)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Step B: factorising the determinant]
With $\Sigma=DRD$, $D=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_N)$ and $R$ the
correlation matrix, multiplicativity of the determinant gives
$\det\Sigma=\det(D)\det(R)\det(D)=\left(\prod_i\sigma_i\right)^2\det R$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Step C: substitution]
$\tfrac12\log\det\Sigma
=\tfrac12\log\!\left[\left(\prod_i\sigma_i\right)^2\det R\right]
=\sum_i\log\sigma_i+\tfrac12\log\det R$, hence
$h(q)=\tfrac N2\log(2\pi e)+\sum_i\log\sigma_i+\tfrac12\log\det R$, which is
equation~\eqref{eq:ceiling}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Sign of the dependence channel.} Hadamard's inequality gives
$\det R\le\prod_i R_{ii}=1$ for a positive semi-definite matrix. A more telling
proof goes through the spectrum: $\det R=\prod_k\lambda_k$ with
$\sum_k\lambda_k=\operatorname{tr}R=N$, and the arithmetic--geometric mean
inequality gives $\prod_k\lambda_k\le\left(\sum_k\lambda_k/N\right)^N=1$, with
equality if and only if all eigenvalues equal~1, that is $R=I$. Hence
$H^{dep}\le0$: \emph{correlation always destroys entropy}.

In the bivariate case, $\det R=1-\rho^2$ and $H^{dep}=\tfrac12\log(1-\rho^2)$;
for $\rho=0.9$ this is $-0.83$~nat. Note that $-H^{dep}$ is then exactly the
mutual information between the two assets: correlation is shared information,
hence destroyed entropy.

\paragraph{Reading note on the tables.} The $H^{cov}$ columns of
Table~\ref{tab:h1} and following report $H^{vol}+H^{dep}$ and \textbf{omit the
constant} $\tfrac N2\log(2\pi e)$, which equals $28.38$~nats for $N=20$. This
constant offset is immaterial since only differences between regimes and
changes over time are interpreted, but it explains why the reported levels are
negative.

\section{The entropy deficit: why $\DD=h(q)-h(p)$}
\label{app:deficit}

The Kullback--Leibler divergence between a law $p$ and a reference law $q$ is
$\KL{p}{q}=\int p\log(p/q)$, positive and zero if and only if $p=q$ almost
everywhere. It is neither symmetric nor equipped with a triangle inequality: it
is not a distance, hence the term divergence. The letters ``KL'' are the
initials of Kullback and Leibler.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:deficit}
If $q=\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)$ has \emph{the first two moments of $p$}, then
$\KL{p}{q}=h(q)-h(p)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\KL{p}{q}=-h(p)-\mathbb{E}_p[\log q(X)]$. Now $\log q$ is exactly quadratic in
$x$, so its expectation depends only on the mean and covariance of the law under
which it is evaluated. Since $p$ and $q$ share these two moments,
$\mathbb{E}_p\!\left[(X-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(X-\mu)\right]=\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma)=N
=\mathbb{E}_q[\cdot]$, hence $\mathbb{E}_p[\log q]=\mathbb{E}_q[\log q]=-h(q)$ and
therefore $\KL{p}{q}=h(q)-h(p)$.
\end{proof}

The moment-matching assumption is essential: without it the identity fails.
This is the whole content of Proposition~\ref{prop:maxent}, read as a
measurement rather than as an existence theorem. Economically, $\DD$ is the
price in nats of the lie ``the market is Gaussian'': the apparent uncertainty
that the Gaussian description overstates, because the true law is more
concentrated than its covariance alone suggests.

\section{The structural index is a divergence}
\label{app:index}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:index}
With $q_i=\mathcal N(\mu_i,\sigma_i^2)$ the Gaussian marginals with the same
scales as $p$,
\[
\KL{p}{\textstyle\prod_i q_i} \;=\; \DD - H^{dep} \;=\; \JJ .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By definition,
\begin{align*}
\KL{p}{\textstyle\prod_i q_i} &= -h(p) - \mathbb{E}_p\!\left[\textstyle\sum_i\log q_i(X_i)\right]
= -h(p) + \sum_i\left[\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma_i^2)+\tfrac12\right]\\
&= -h(p) + \sum_i\log\sigma_i + \tfrac N2\log(2\pi e),
\end{align*}
using $\mathbb{E}_p[(X_i-\mu_i)^2]=\sigma_i^2$, true by construction.
Substituting equation~\eqref{eq:three}, i.e.\
$h(p)=\tfrac N2\log(2\pi e)+\sum_i\log\sigma_i+H^{dep}-\DD$, the scale terms and
the constant telescope and $\DD-H^{dep}$ remains.
\end{proof}

This is an exact identity, not an approximation, and it is what justifies
Definition~\ref{def:J}. Removing the scale channel from \eqref{eq:three} might
look like a fudge; the object that remains turns out to be a Kullback--Leibler
divergence in good standing, and therefore inherits three properties for free:
it is positive; it is zero if and only if returns are independent \emph{and}
Gaussian, which provides a well-defined rather than arbitrary reference point;
and it is invariant under asset-by-asset changes of units, since $p$ and
$\prod_i q_i$ transform in the same way.

It is useful to set this identity against the general chain rule
$\KL{p}{\prod_i q_i}=\sum_i\KL{p_i}{q_i}+I(x)$, where $I(x)=\KL{p}{\prod_i p_i}$
is the multi-information. The first term is the sum of marginal deficits
$\sum_i\Dun(x_i)$, the second the total dependence. The estimator used in the
paper estimates the first term with the approximation of \citet{hyvarinen1998}
and replaces the second by its linear part $-H^{dep}$; nonlinear tail
dependence is therefore omitted from $\JJ$ and measured separately through a
co-exceedance rate.

\section{Mutual information: the two propositions and an example}
\label{app:mi}

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:conditioning}.} By definition of
conditional entropy, $\mathbb{E}_Y[h(X\mid Y)]=h(X,Y)-h(Y)$, and by
\eqref{eq:mi}, $I(X;Y)=h(X)+h(Y)-h(X,Y)$. Adding the two,
$\mathbb{E}_Y[h(X\mid Y)]=h(X)-I(X;Y)$. Positivity of $I$ follows from that of
the Kullback--Leibler divergence, of which it is a special case, with equality
if and only if $p_{XY}=p_X\otimes p_Y$.

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:constraint}.} Immediate: an
additional constraint can only shrink the feasible set, and the supremum of a
functional over a smaller set cannot increase.

\paragraph{Why the two are not equivalent.} The first quantifies but holds only
on average; the second holds realisation by realisation but concerns the
ceiling and quantifies nothing. A discrete counterexample suffices to show that
a particular realisation can \emph{increase} entropy. Let $X$ be binary with
$\mathbb{P}(X=a)=0.99$, with entropy $H(X)=0.056$~nat. Let a signal $Y$, with
probability $0.98$, remove all doubt ($H(X\mid Y=0)=0$), and with probability
$0.02$ lead to $\mathbb{P}(X=a\mid Y=1)=1/2$, i.e.\ $H(X\mid Y=1)=\log 2=0.693$~nat
--- twelve times the prior entropy. The average is nevertheless
$0.98\times0+0.02\times0.693=0.014$~nat, well below $0.056$, and
$I(X;Y)=0.042$~nat. This is exactly why the paper predicts a jump component that
is \emph{mainly}, not exclusively, one-sided.

\paragraph{Worked Gaussian example.} Let $X\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ be
tomorrow's return and $Y=X+\varepsilon$ a noisy signal,
$\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_\varepsilon^2)$ independent. Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mi-gauss}
I(X;Y)=\tfrac12\log\!\left(1+\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma_\varepsilon^2}\right),
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)=\frac{\sigma^2\sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\sigma^2+\sigma_\varepsilon^2},
\end{equation}
the posterior variance not depending on the realisation $y$. With
$\sigma=1\%$ per day:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
Signal quality & $\sigma_\varepsilon$ & $I(X;Y)$ (nats) & posterior vol. & $\sqrt{2\eta}$ equivalent \\
\midrule
sharp   & $0.5\%$ & $0.805$ & $0.45\%$ & $1.27\sigma$ \\
medium  & $1.0\%$ & $0.347$ & $0.71\%$ & $0.83\sigma$ \\
vague   & $3.0\%$ & $0.053$ & $0.95\%$ & $0.32\sigma$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A factor of fifteen separates the size of the entropy jump caused by decisive
news from that caused by a rumour. The last column translates each jump onto
the severity scale of Section~\ref{sec:stress}: it is the directional move that
a scenario of the same radius would produce.

\section{Exploratory: composition of crises and of scenarios}
\label{app:explo}

This appendix gathers a result that clears only one of the two nulls and the
use made of it to compose a stress scenario. We separate it from the body of the
paper because nothing above depends on it, and because it does not meet the
standard the paper sets itself everywhere else. It should be read as a lead,
not as a result.

\subsection{H8: the composition of the crisis budget}
\label{sec:h8}

The seven hypotheses above treat each channel on average over a whole regime.
The question here is different: \emph{inside a given crisis}, which channel does
the work? A jump detected in $\JJ$ is rarely an isolated event --- it is part of
a run of nearby jumps, as Figure~\ref{fig:event} already shows --- so we first
merge jumps less than 40 trading days apart into \emph{episodes}, then
decompose each episode's move, from onset to peak, over the three channels:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:episode-budget}
\Delta\JJ_{\text{episode}} \;=\; \underbrace{\Delta(-H^{dep})}_{\text{dependence}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\Delta d_{\text{odd}}}_{\text{skewness}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\Delta d_{\text{even}}}_{\text{tail}} ,
\end{equation}
an exact identity from Definition~\ref{def:J} and the odd/even decomposition of
negentropy behind \eqref{eq:edgeworth} (\texttt{episode\_\allowbreak{}channel\_\allowbreak{}budget},
\texttt{src/\allowbreak{}jumps.py}): the three shares sum to one by construction, so this is
not an approximation of a per-crisis budget but the budget itself.

\paragraph{The raw fact.} On all three panels, the \emph{skewness} channel is
almost never the dominant contributor (1 episode in 139, all panels combined)
--- one more piece of evidence against P\ref{p:sign} as the dominant mechanism,
on top of the already marginal result of H5. The split between
\emph{dependence} and \emph{tail}, on the other hand, looks like a real
typology: on the main panel, dependence dominates $46\%$ of the 65 detected
episodes; on Kenneth French's 49-industry panel, $79\%$ of 61 episodes; on
\texttt{EuStockMarkets}, $69\%$ of 13 episodes.

\subsection{Calibrated composition: which channel pays for severity}
\label{sec:composition}

Construction~\eqref{eq:ball} constrains only the projection of the portfolio
onto the KL ball --- it fixes the stressed portfolio mean and volatility, $m$ and
$s$ --- and is silent on how the underlying covariance should deform, asset by
asset, to reach them. The simplest choice compatible with that constraint, the
one that produces Tables~\ref{tab:ladder} and~\ref{tab:classical}, is a uniform
dilation: every marginal volatility multiplied by the same factor $v$,
correlations unchanged. That is a construction default, not a statement about
how crises behave. H1 says how they behave: the dependence channel moves on its
own account, and it is the only regime channel in the whole paper to clear both
nulls (Table~\ref{tab:h1}). H8 refines that fact at the episode scale, with the
narrowed scope its own null demands (Section~\ref{sec:h8}). This section moves
these two facts from the ``result'' column to the ``calibration'' column, and
relies exclusively on the two hypotheses their respective nulls retain.

\paragraph{The mixture stays exact.}
\begin{proposition}[One severity, several compositions]
\label{prop:composition}
Let $\Sigma_a,\Sigma_b\succeq0$ be two covariances and $w$ a vector of portfolio
weights such that $w^\top\Sigma_a w = w^\top\Sigma_b w = s^2$. Then for any
$\alpha\in[0,1]$, $\Sigma_\alpha \equiv (1-\alpha)\Sigma_a+\alpha\Sigma_b$ still
satisfies $w^\top\Sigma_\alpha w=s^2$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$w^\top\Sigma w$ is linear in $\Sigma$, so
$w^\top\Sigma_\alpha w = (1-\alpha)\,w^\top\Sigma_a w + \alpha\,w^\top\Sigma_b w
= (1-\alpha)s^2+\alpha s^2=s^2$.
\end{proof}
An immediate and usable consequence: one can vary \emph{how} the severity $s$ is
distributed across assets without ever changing $s$. The mixture parameter,
$\alpha\in[0,1]$, is what we call the \emph{dependence share}.

\paragraph{A physical ceiling.} Correlation alone cannot carry any severity. At
fixed marginal volatilities $\sigma_i$,
$w^\top\Sigma w = \sum_i w_i^2\sigma_i^2 + \sum_{i\ne j}w_iw_j\sigma_i\sigma_j\rho_{ij}$
is maximised, over valid correlation matrices, by $\rho_{ij}=1$ for all
$i\ne j$ --- perfect dependence --- where it equals
$\left(\sum_i w_i\sigma_i\right)^2$. On the main panel, this ceiling is only
about $1.5$~times today's volatility: a diversified portfolio of twenty stocks
simply cannot reach high severity through correlation alone, however
``dependent'' the underlying crisis --- which Table~\ref{tab:ladder} shows on
this panel: the $1.37$ multiplier of the 5-year rung is still reachable through
correlation alone, the $1.63$ of the 10-year rung is not (implied average
correlation of $1.1$). The construction retained accounts for this: correlation
is pushed towards its ceiling ($\rho=1$, capped) in proportion to the requested
dependence share, and the remaining gap, unavoidable beyond that ceiling, is
filled by a final uniform dilation, so that Proposition~\ref{prop:composition}
applies whatever the requested share (\texttt{calibrated\_\allowbreak{}covariance},
\texttt{src/\allowbreak{}stress.py}).

\paragraph{Where the dependence share comes from.} Two sources, neither
arbitrary.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{H1 anchor.} The regime share of Table~\ref{tab:h1}:
$|{-0.99}|/(|{-0.99}|+|5.97|)=0.14$. It is the most robust fact in the whole
paper --- it clears both nulls --- turned into a calibration parameter instead of
remaining a result that is quoted and then dropped.
\item \textbf{H8 refinement.} The same share, measured only on the more severe
half of detected episodes: $0.49$, more than three times the regime average ---
consistent with Table~\ref{tab:severityq}, where quintiles~4 and~5 clearly
dominate quintile~2, without claiming a monotone law.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What this changes, at fixed severity.} Table~\ref{tab:composition}
prices the 10-year scenario of Table~\ref{tab:ladder} under these compositions.
The price in \emph{ambient} nats --- the full KL divergence between the
stressed scenario and today's distribution, computable in full dimension, not to
be confused with the radius $\eta$ which constrains only the projection ---
\emph{decreases} as the dependence share increases, rather than increasing. This
is not a computational anomaly: uniform dilation is the cheapest choice only in
the sense of the projected constraint; it is not a proven minimum of the ambient
divergence. That minimum exists --- the first-order conditions of the problem
``minimise $\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_1\Sigma_0^{-1})-\log\det\Sigma_1$ subject to
$w^\top\Sigma_1w=s^2$'' give a rank-one perturbation of the \emph{precision}
matrix, $\Sigma_1^{-1}=\Sigma_0^{-1}-\lambda\,ww^\top$ --- but that object is not
interpretable asset by asset and is not used here. The empirical finding is
therefore this: on this panel, moving towards the historically observed
composition also moves, without this being sought, towards that unused
minimum. A risk manager who assumes uniform dilation by default is not
protecting themselves by being prudent --- the composition anchored on H1 and
refined by H8 is both more realistic \emph{and} cheaper on the market's
information scale.
