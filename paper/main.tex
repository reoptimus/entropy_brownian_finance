\title{Measuring the severity of stress scenarios in nats:\\
what entropy offers to stress-test designers}
\author{Working version}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Authorities that design stress scenarios, such as the adverse
scenario of the EU-wide stress test coordinated by the European Banking
Authority (EBA), must answer two questions: is the scenario severe enough, and
is it still plausible? This paper proposes to measure both on a single scale,
entropy, expressed in nats. A nat is a quantity of information. For a pure
shift of the mean, a scenario of $\eta$~nats is a shock of $\sqrt{2\eta}$
standard deviations: one nat buys $1.4$ standard deviations. We apply this
scale to 33~years of returns on twenty US stocks, to Kenneth French's
48~industry portfolios, and to a euro-area panel covering the five segments of
the ECB's Composite Indicator of Systemic Stress (CISS). Three results stand
out. \textbf{(1)} A simple robustness test, which reruns every computation on
data whose time order has been shuffled, removes most of the crisis signals
that entropy appears to show: they are artefacts of estimation. One fact
survives on every panel: under stress, markets couple, meaning that the number
of independent sources of risk falls. \textbf{(2)} Measured in nats, classical
scenarios that shock volatilities, correlations and levels separately pay
$15$ to $33\,\%$ too much for their tail loss, and the split of the shock
between volatility and correlation can be calibrated on history instead of
being assumed. \textbf{(3)} The coupling measure decomposes exactly across
market segments. In the euro area it flags recessions as well as the CISS
does and shows which segments stress ties together, but it does not forecast
growth.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Two facts about financial prices sit uneasily together. The first is that the
Gaussian model is the natural answer to a question of ignorance: fix a mean and
a variance, know nothing else, and the maximum-entropy distribution is
Gaussian. The second is that returns are not Gaussian: they have fat tails,
negative skewness, and their dependence tightens precisely when it matters.

The usual reaction treats the second fact as a defect of the first model and
patches it: add jumps to the price, add a stochastic volatility factor, add a
copula. This paper goes the other way. If the Gaussian is what maximum entropy
delivers, then any departure from the Gaussian measures how far the market is
from maximal ignorance, that is, a measure of information. The question is
therefore not how to patch the Gaussian, but how to read the size, dynamics and
direction of the departure.

The paper is organised around an asymmetry in that departure. Information
arrives in bursts and is absorbed gradually. A central-bank surprise, a
default, a war removes uncertainty in an instant; the market then spends weeks
working out the implications, and uncertainty rebuilds. If entropy is the right
currency of uncertainty, its dynamics should be a sawtooth: sharp drops, slow
recoveries. Half of that shape is a theorem: receiving information cannot, on
average, increase uncertainty. The other half, the slow recovery, is a
hypothesis that we test.

\paragraph{Who this paper is for.} It is written first for institutions that
design stress scenarios for others: the European Systemic Risk Board (ESRB)
and the ECB, which write the adverse scenario of the EBA stress test, national
central banks, the IMF in its financial sector assessment programmes, and
insurance supervisors. These institutions must produce scenarios that are
``severe but plausible'' \citep{bcbs2018}. In practice severity is measured in
losses and plausibility is judged by experts. A common unit for both is
missing. Entropy provides one: it measures in nats the distance between a
scenario and the current state of the market, whatever the asset class, and it
reduces to standard deviations in simple cases. Judging the plausibility of a
scenario by a statistical distance is not new
\citep{breuer2009,breuer2013,glasserman2014}. What we add is a calibration of
that distance on data, a test that separates real signals from artefacts, and
an application to the euro area.

\paragraph{Four contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A measurement framework} (section~\ref{sec:cadre}). The
uncertainty of a return vector splits exactly into three channels: scale
(volatilities), dependence (correlations) and shape (skewness and fat tails).
Removing scale leaves a unit-free index, the structural index $\JJ$, which
measures the market's distance from maximal ignorance.
\item \emph{A robustness test} (sections~\ref{sec:methode}
and~\ref{sec:resultats}). An entropy measure estimated on real data jumps up
and decays slowly even when no information arrives: this is a mechanical
effect of estimation. We therefore judge every result against \emph{placebo
nulls}, that is, against the same computations run on resampled data. Only
one result survives everywhere: under stress, markets couple.
\item \emph{A severity unit for scenarios} (section~\ref{sec:stress}). A
scenario of severity $\eta$ is the worst distribution within $\eta$~nats of
the current distribution. The radius $\eta$ is calibrated on how fast the
market itself revises its distribution. The same unit can audit an existing
scenario and choose how to split the shock between volatility and correlation.
\item \emph{A monitoring indicator} (section~\ref{sec:cb}). The coupling
measure decomposes exactly across market segments. Applied to the five
segments of the ECB's CISS, it shows which segments stress ties together,
which directly informs the correlation structure of a scenario.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{A warning.} The structural index is computed from estimated
moments (variances, correlations, skewness, kurtosis). On fat-tailed data,
estimation noise pushes such an index \emph{up} abruptly and lets it come down
\emph{slowly}. That is exactly the sawtooth the theory predicts. The result is
uncomfortable and, we think, the most useful thing in the paper: most of the
signals that look overwhelming do not survive the null. Anyone building a
crisis indicator from estimated moments should run this null before believing
their own chart.

\paragraph{Outline.} Section~\ref{sec:concepts} defines the concepts used.
Section~\ref{sec:cadre} presents the framework and the eight hypotheses
tested. Section~\ref{sec:methode} describes the data and the placebo test,
section~\ref{sec:resultats} the results. Sections~\ref{sec:stress}
and~\ref{sec:cb} present the two applications: measuring scenario severity
and the entropic CISS. Section~\ref{sec:reco} summarises recommendations and
limitations. Technical justifications are in the appendix; full proofs are in
a separate theoretical supplement.

\section{The concepts used}
\label{sec:concepts}

This section defines, in the order they are needed, every concept the rest of
the paper uses. No calculation is needed to follow what comes next.

\begin{description}
\item[Entropy.] The entropy $h(p)$ of a distribution $p$ measures its degree of
uncertainty: the more spread out the distribution, the higher the entropy. For
a continuous variable, $h(p)=-\int p\log p$. Its level depends on units
(expressing returns in percent rather than decimals shifts it by a constant):
only its \emph{changes} and the \emph{divergences} below have absolute
meaning.

\item[Nat.] The unit of information obtained with the natural logarithm.
$1$~nat $\approx 1.44$~bit. All quantities in the paper are in nats.

\item[Maximum entropy.] Among all distributions with a given mean and
covariance, the Gaussian has maximal entropy: it is the distribution that
assumes the least beyond those two moments. Any departure from the Gaussian is
therefore additional information.

\item[Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence.] $\KL{p}{q}=\int p\log(p/q)\ge0$
measures in nats the gap between a distribution $p$ and a reference
distribution $q$. It is zero if and only if $p=q$ and does not depend on
units. It is not a distance (it is not symmetric), hence the word
``divergence''. Practical reading: if two Gaussians share the covariance
$\Sigma$ and have means $\mu_0$ and $\mu_1$,
$\KL{p_1}{p_0}=\tfrac12(\mu_1-\mu_0)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_0)$, half the
squared Mahalanobis distance. A shock of $k$ standard deviations therefore
costs $k^2/2$~nats.

\item[Mutual information.] $I(X;Y)$ is the KL divergence between the joint law
of $X$ and $Y$ and the product of their separate laws. It measures what $X$
and $Y$ share and is zero if they are independent. For two Gaussian variables
with correlation $\rho$, $I=-\tfrac12\log(1-\rho^2)$: $0.83$~nat for
$\rho=0.9$.

\item[Total correlation (coupling).] For $N$ assets with correlation matrix
$R$, $C(R)=-\tfrac12\log\det R\ge0$ extends mutual information to more than two
variables \citep{watanabe1960}. It is zero if the assets are independent and
rises when they start to move together. We call it \emph{coupling}. An
equivalent reading is the \emph{effective number of risk modes}, which equals
$N$ when assets are independent and tends to $1$ when a single common factor
explains everything.

\item[The three entropy channels.] The entropy of a return vector splits
exactly into three terms (section~\ref{sec:cadre}): \emph{scale} (the level of
volatilities), \emph{dependence} (coupling, counted negatively) and
\emph{shape} (the deficit defined below).

\item[Entropy deficit $\DD$.] The KL divergence between the true distribution
and the Gaussian with the same mean and covariance. It measures what the
Gaussian misses: skewness and fat tails.

\item[Structural index $\JJ$.] The sum of shape and dependence, $\JJ=\DD+C$.
It is the KL divergence between the true distribution and independent
Gaussians with the same volatilities. It is zero if returns are Gaussian and
independent, and it does not depend on the level of volatility.

\item[Stress regime.] Dates on which 21-day market volatility exceeds the
threshold of the top $10\,\%$ observed \emph{up to that date}. The threshold
never uses future information.

\item[Placebo null.] The same computations, rerun on data whose time order
has been shuffled. The \emph{i.i.d.} null draws days one by one: it keeps fat
tails and cross-asset correlation, and destroys all dynamics. The
\emph{block} null draws 21-day blocks: it also keeps the clustering of
turbulent days, and destroys only longer-run dynamics. Each null is repeated
200~times. A result \emph{passes} a null if at most $5\,\%$ of the
200~repetitions produce a result as strong as the real data.

\item[Information flow.] $I^{(h)}_t=\KL{p_t}{p_{t-h}}$: by how many nats the
market has revised its own distribution over $h$ days.

\item[Scenario of severity $\eta$ and return period.] The worst distribution
within $\eta$~nats of the current distribution. It is assigned the return
period of an information flow of the same size: a radius the market travels
in one month only once a decade is a ``ten-year'' scenario.

\item[Tail loss (ES$_{99}$).] The $99\,\%$ expected shortfall: the average
loss on the worst $1\,\%$ of days.
\end{description}

\section{The framework: from maximal ignorance to three channels}
\label{sec:cadre}

\subsection{Why the Gaussian is the reference}

The step from ``maximum entropy gives a Gaussian'' to ``prices are Gaussian''
is often asserted and rarely argued, because maximum entropy takes the variance
as given without saying where it comes from. Appendix~\ref{app:pont} closes
that bridge. If time is measured in traded volume rather than in days, volume
fixes the variance, and the least committal distribution of the price
increment is Gaussian. This is the mixture-of-distributions hypothesis
\citep{clark1973,ane2000}, derived rather than assumed. In calendar time,
returns depart from the Gaussian for two reasons: the volume clock is random,
or information constrains the market. No empirical result in the paper depends
on this bridge; it motivates the Gaussian as the zero point.

\subsection{Three channels and the structural index}

Write the covariance $\Sigma=DRD$, where $D$ holds the volatilities $\sigma_i$
and $R$ the correlations. The entropy of the true distribution $p$ splits
exactly (appendix~\ref{app:preuves}):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:three}
h(p) \;=\; \tfrac{N}{2}\log(2\pi e)
\;+\; \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_i \log\sigma_i}_{\text{scale}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac12\log\det R}_{\text{dependence }(=-C)}
\;-\; \underbrace{\DD}_{\text{shape}} .
\end{equation}
The first two terms form the entropy of the Gaussian with the same covariance,
the ceiling. The deficit $\DD$ says how far below it the true distribution
lies.

The scale channel is a problem: it rises with volatility. In a crisis, entropy
computed from the covariance matrix alone \emph{rises} (by $5$~nats on our main
panel), whereas intuition says a crisis is a moment when information
constrains the market. Using the level of Gaussian entropy as a stress
indicator therefore gets the sign wrong. We remove that channel and keep the
other two:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:J}
\JJ \;=\; \DD \;+\; C \;=\; \KL{p}{\textstyle\prod_i q_i} \;\ge\; 0 ,
\end{equation}
where the $q_i$ are Gaussians with each asset's volatility. $\JJ$ is a genuine
divergence (appendix~\ref{app:preuves}): it is positive, zero if and only if
returns are Gaussian and independent, and unit-free. It rises when assets
couple ($C$) or when their distributions depart from the Gaussian ($\DD$), and
one can always say which part of a move comes from each channel.

\subsection{What the framework predicts, and the eight hypotheses}

News reduces uncertainty. Formally, receiving a signal $Y$ lowers the
\emph{average} entropy of the return $X$ by exactly the mutual information
$I(X;Y)$ \citep{cover2006}. The entropy jump therefore has a size measurable in
nats: the information content of the news. The framework adds two postulates,
detailed in appendix~\ref{app:dynamique}. The first (\emph{relaxation}) says
that, absent new information, $\JJ$ slowly returns to its normal level. The
second (\emph{sign}) says that jumps come with negative skewness, because $\JJ$
alone cannot tell a crash from a rally. The result is a sawtooth: $\JJ$ rises
by jumps and comes down slowly. The framework also predicts that important
news, because it hits every asset on the same day, raises coupling and tail
thickness together.

\section{Data and method}
\label{sec:methode}

\subsection{Data}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Main panel:} twenty individual S\&P~500 stocks, daily, 1990--2022
(8,312 days), covering the Asian crisis, LTCM, the dot-com bubble, 2008, the
sovereign debt crisis, COVID-19 and 2022. Individual stocks rather than
portfolios, because sector aggregation mechanically creates dependence.
\item \emph{Industry panel:} Kenneth French's 48 daily industry portfolios,
1990--2026 (about 9,000 days).
\item \emph{Control panel:} the DAX, SMI, CAC and FTSE indices, 1991--1998
\citep{bollerslev1996}, too small to carry a result on its own.
\item \emph{Euro-area panel:} 28 daily series across the five CISS segments,
2005--2026 (section~\ref{sec:cb}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Estimation}

Correlations and volatilities are estimated on a 252-day rolling window with
the \citet{ledoit2004} correction, or with exponential weighting (60-day
half-life) for everything to do with jumps. The shape deficit is estimated
asset by asset with the \citet{hyvarinen1998} approximation, accurate to a few
percent on fat-tailed laws like those of daily returns. A jump is flagged when
the daily change in $\JJ$ exceeds four times a local scale that is robust to
jumps \citep{bns2004}. Regime comparisons use a 252-day block bootstrap;
forecasts are evaluated out of sample with the \citet{dm1995} test.
Appendix~\ref{app:details} gives the details.

\subsection{Why a placebo null is indispensable}
\label{sec:placebo}

The index $\JJ$ is a convex, positive function of estimated moments. This
creates a trap. When an extreme day enters the estimation window, the index
jumps up; it then comes down slowly as that day loses weight. This movement
mimics exactly the sawtooth the theory predicts, without any information
having arrived.

A simulation shows it. Feed independent Gaussian returns with constant
covariance through the same pipeline: a world without any dynamics. A single
injected stress day raises $\JJ$ by $2.1$~nats, and the index decays with a
half-life of $35$~days, through estimator memory alone. With no event at all,
the detector finds $37$ positive jumps and no negative one.

A result is therefore evidence only if it exceeds what data without dynamics
produce. Hence the two nulls defined in section~\ref{sec:concepts}. They run
exactly the same pipeline, including the definition of the stress regime. They
answer two different questions. The i.i.d.\ null asks: beyond the fact that
turbulent periods contain large days, does the measure detect anything? The
block null asks the harder question: beyond the mere clustering of turbulent
days, does it detect dynamics specific to crises? A result that passes both is
established. A result that passes only one is not identified.

\subsection{The fact that survives: under stress, markets couple}

Under stress, coupling among the twenty stocks rises by $0.99$~nat. The i.i.d.\
null produces only $0.21$~nat (none of the 200 repetitions reaches the
observed value), the block null $0.51$~nat ($p=0.03$). Concretely, the
effective number of independent risk sources falls from $13.1$ to $11.4$ out
of $20$. The same result holds on French's 48 industries: $3.65$~nats against
$0.56$ and $2.46$ ($p=0.025$). It is the only result common to both panels. Its
relative strength is similar on individual stocks and on industries, which
rules out an aggregation artefact.

The second result that passes both nulls on the main panel is the
\emph{range} of $\JJ$: the index travels $18.95$~nats over the sample,
against $10.05$ and $13.77$ in the nulls ($p=0.035$ against blocks). Real
markets move the index further than any resampling of the same returns. This
result does not, however, replicate against the block null on industries.

\paragraph{Coupling is not just a volatility effect.} Three checks, in
table~\ref{tab:cb-measures}, rule out the usual objections.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Volatility.} Measured correlations rise mechanically when variances
rise \citep{forbes2002}. We therefore divide each return by its recent
volatility before computing correlations (``devolatilisation''). Coupling falls
by about $30\,\%$ but still passes both nulls on both panels.
\item \emph{Extreme days.} We replace each return by its rank, converted to a
Gaussian score, which makes the measure insensitive to extreme values. This
``rank'' version is the most robust of the three.
\item \emph{Existing measures.} The absorption ratio of \citet{kritzman2011},
which measures the share of variance explained by the first factors, fails the
block null on both panels. The likely reason is that $\log\det R$ accounts for
the whole spectrum of the correlation matrix, including the small eigenvalues
that collapse when assets couple.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What does not survive}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{One-sided jumps (H3).} The detector finds 158 upward jumps against
9 downward, and the chart of $\JJ$ is visibly a sawtooth
(figure~\ref{fig:index} in the appendix). But the i.i.d.\ null produces as
many: 148 up and 3 down on average, with stronger asymmetry than the data. The
sawtooth is what a fat-tailed law produces when passed through the estimator,
whether or not information arrives in bursts.
\item \emph{Shape under stress (H1, $\DD$).} Tails thicken under stress
($+0.27$~nat), but less than in the nulls ($+1.25$ and $+0.65$). A ``stress''
window in a shuffled world contains isolated extreme days, which thicken the
measured tails more.
\item \emph{Relaxation (H4).} The measured half-life, $121$~days, lies between
that of the i.i.d.\ null ($92$) and that of the block null ($163$). It is
therefore not identified: $121$~days cannot be read as an absorption time for
shocks.
\item \emph{Sign (H5).} Jumps of $\JJ$ do coincide with negative skewness
(correlation $-0.24$), but the nulls produce the same effect in $8$ to
$10\,\%$ of cases, above the $5\,\%$ threshold.
\item \emph{Channel coupling (H6) and compensation (H2).} Dependence and tails
move together under stress ($0.63$ against $0.04$ in calm markets), but the
i.i.d.\ null couples them even more ($0.90$). A window containing a large
common day shows both higher correlation and thicker tails, real or shuffled.
The same mechanism produces the compensation slope of H2.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Entropy describes, it does not forecast (H7)}

The question is: does knowing today's entropy say anything about risk over the
next three weeks that today's volatility does not already say? We compare a
model using volatility only with a model that adds an entropy channel. Both are
estimated on the past only, re-estimated each year, and compared on dates they
have not seen. No channel improves the forecast of volatility, drawdown or
worst loss: the best gain is $3.3\,\%$ in squared error, with $p=0.23$. In the
estimation sample, by contrast, every coefficient looks highly significant
($p<10^{-4}$): the classic trap of overlapping windows. Entropy measures are
therefore \emph{diagnostic}, not predictive.

\subsection{How crises split their intensity (H8)}
\label{sec:h8}

The previous hypotheses compare averages over a whole regime. H8 looks at each
crisis. We merge jumps less than 40 days apart into \emph{episodes}, and split
the rise of $\JJ$ in each episode exactly into dependence, skewness and tails.
Skewness almost never dominates (1 episode out of 139). The split between
dependence and tails varies: dependence dominates $46\,\%$ of the 65 episodes
on the main panel, against $26\,\%$ on average under the i.i.d.\ null (range
$18$--$33\,\%$); $79\,\%$ of the 61 industry episodes, against $43\,\%$.

Against the block null, however, this result is not identified once blocks
reach 21~days (table~\ref{tab:blocksens} in the appendix). We therefore draw
no conclusion about the nature of crises. We use it for one purpose only, a
more modest one: calibrating the composition of a stress scenario
(section~\ref{sec:composition}), which only requires the observed composition
to be more structured than that of a memoryless world. The size of an episode
does not predict its composition (rank correlation $0.05$).

\paragraph{The takeaway.} What distinguishes a real crisis from a series of
large days is not the thickening of each asset's tails; it is that assets
couple. For a scenario designer, this means that the dependence structure of a
scenario deserves as much attention as the level of the shocks, and that any
crisis indicator based on estimated moments should be confronted with a null
before use.

\section{Application 1: measuring and calibrating scenario severity}
\label{sec:stress}

This section does not depend on any of H1--H7: it is a measurement convention
plus an optimisation. It speaks directly to the design of market scenarios,
such as the financial shocks of the European adverse scenario or the global
market shocks of US stress tests.

\subsection{An entropy-ball scenario}

\begin{definition}[Scenario of severity $\eta$]
Let $p_0$ be the current distribution of returns and $\rho$ a risk measure
(for example a portfolio's ES$_{99}$). The scenario of severity $\eta$ is the
most adverse distribution within $\eta$~nats of $p_0$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ball}
p^\star_\eta \;=\; \arg\max_{p\,:\,\KL{p}{p_0}\,\le\,\eta} \rho(p).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The construction is known \citep{breuer2013,glasserman2014}. For a portfolio
and a Gaussian reference it has an explicit solution: the scenario shifts the
portfolio mean down and raises its volatility, both drawn from the same budget
of $\eta$~nats. The simplest case gives the exchange rate between information
and risk:
\begin{equation}
\text{a purely directional shock of }\eta\text{ nats is a move of }\sqrt{2\eta}\text{ standard deviations.}
\end{equation}
One nat buys $1.41$ standard deviations, two nats buy two, and a
three-standard-deviation shock costs $4.5$~nats. The severity parameter is
therefore not an abstraction: it translates directly into the language of
committees.

\subsection{Calibrating the radius on the market itself}

It remains to choose $\eta$. We read it off the market's information flow,
that is, off how much the market revises its own distribution over 21 days,
$I^{(21)}_t=\KL{p_t}{p_{t-21}}$, measured on the same object as the constraint
(the portfolio's distribution). The historical quantiles of this flow give a
radius for each return period (table~\ref{tab:ladder}).

information flow, measured on smoothed estimators, understates the true
revision; or a Gaussian ball is not the right geometry for volatility shocks
this abrupt. We do not decide between the two. The return periods in the
table should therefore not be quoted as frequencies. What survives this
limitation are the \emph{relative} comparisons of the next two sections.

\subsection{Auditing an existing scenario}

The same unit prices a scenario built by expert judgement. Take the type of
scenario common in stress exercises: every volatility multiplied by the same
factor, every correlation forced to a high value, and a directional shock of

$\KL{p_{\text{scenario}}}{p_0}$, is available in closed form. It can then be
compared with the entropy-ball scenario that produces \emph{the same tail

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The classical scenario is too expensive.} For the same tail loss

portfolio does not see.
\item \emph{The expensive component is the uniform dilation of volatilities.}
Shifting the mean by three standard deviations costs $4.5$~nats. Doubling every
volatility costs $16.1$~nats on its own, more than the whole scenario.
\item \emph{Volatility and correlation pay for each other.} The three
components cost $28.7$~nats separately but $10.0$ together. Forcing
correlations up concentrates risk on a single market factor, and dilating
that factor is far cheaper than dilating every direction. This is exactly what
observed crises do: they load risk onto a few common factors
(section~\ref{sec:resultats}). A scenario that shocks volatility and
correlation as two separate dials cannot represent it.
\end{itemize}
For an authority, the practical value is the ability to \emph{audit} an
existing scenario without replacing it: each scenario receives a cost in nats,
comparable across exercises and asset classes, and one can check that it does
not buy its severity through a needlessly improbable route.

\subsection{Composing a scenario: volatility or correlation?}
\label{sec:composition}

The entropy ball fixes the severity \emph{of the portfolio} (its stressed mean
and volatility). It does not say how each asset's covariance should move to get
there. The default used so far is a uniform dilation of volatilities with
correlations unchanged. That is a convention, not a fact about crises. Yet
section~\ref{sec:resultats} shows that, under stress, dependence moves on its
own.

A simple property lets us account for this without changing severity. A
portfolio's variance, $w^\top\Sigma w$, is linear in $\Sigma$. If two
covariances give the same portfolio variance, any mixture of the two does too
(proposition in appendix~\ref{app:preuves}). One can therefore mix an
``all-volatility'' covariance and an ``all-correlation'' covariance with a
weight $\alpha$, the \emph{dependence share}, without touching severity. One
caveat: correlation alone cannot carry any severity. With every correlation
equal to one, our portfolio's volatility cannot exceed $1.5$ times its current
level; beyond that, the rest comes from dilation.

The dependence share is calibrated on the robust results. The \emph{anchor}
comes from H1: under stress, dependence accounts for $0.99/(0.99+5.97)=14\,\%$
of the move in Gaussian entropy (scale plus dependence). The
\emph{refinement} comes from H8: on the more severe half of episodes, that
share reaches $49\,\%$.

finding is specific to this panel and should not be read as a general law.

\subsection{Reverse stress testing}

The same unit also answers the reverse question: what is the least improbable
scenario that produces a given loss? On the main panel, the daily loss exceeded
one day in a hundred ($3.2\,\%$) costs $1.5$~nats; the one exceeded one day in
a thousand ($7.7\,\%$) costs $4.8$~nats; the worst day in 33 years
($11.5\,\%$) costs $9.0$~nats. Beyond a few nats, the non-parametric version of
this calculation rests on a handful of days only, and the Gaussian version
should be preferred.

\section{Application 2: an entropic CISS}
\label{sec:cb}

The paper's only robust result, coupling under stress, becomes here an
indicator in the format of the ECB's CISS \citep{hollo2012}. Like the CISS, it
covers the money-market, bond, equity, financial-intermediary and foreign
exchange segments. Unlike the CISS, it says what part of the coupling lies
within each segment and what part lies \emph{between} segments. For a scenario
designer, this is precisely the information needed to set the correlations
between shocks to different markets.

\subsection{An exact decomposition by segment}

Split the series into segments $B_1,\dots,B_K$. Total coupling decomposes
exactly:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:blocks}
C(R) \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{K} C(R_{kk})}_{\text{within segments}}
\;+\; \underbrace{I(B_1;\dots;B_K)}_{\text{between segments}},
\end{equation}
where $R_{kk}$ is the correlation matrix of segment $k$. The between-segment
term is zero exactly when segments move independently of one another: it is
the systemic part. For two segments $A$ and $B$, the mutual information
$I(A;B)$ gives a contagion map in the same unit. Everything is unit-free:
equity returns, rate changes and exchange rates can sit in the same matrix.
We use the rank version of coupling.

\subsection{The euro-area panel}

The panel is daily, from January 2005 to October 2026 (5,690 days), with 28
series: the overnight rate (EONIA, then \texteuro{}STR) for the money market;
six ECB sovereign yields and spreads for bonds; five national indices for
equities; nine banks and three insurers for intermediaries; four euro exchange
rates. For the money market the CISS uses the Euribor minus overnight spread;
since Euribor is not available on the ECB data portal, this segment currently
contains the overnight rate only.

measures, SRISK \citep{brownlees2017} and $\Delta$CoVaR \citep{adrian2016}.

\section{Recommendations and limitations}
\label{sec:reco}

\subsection{Recommendations for scenario designers}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Price every scenario in nats.} The cost
$\KL{p_{\text{scenario}}}{p_0}$ is available in closed form for a Gaussian
scenario. It makes scenarios on different markets and in different exercises
comparable, and it reads in standard deviations ($k$ standard deviations
$=k^2/2$ nats).
\item \emph{Check efficiency before severity.} Compare a scenario's cost with
that of the entropic scenario with the same loss: an overpayment of $15$ to
$33\,\%$, as for the classical scenarios tested here, signals severity bought
through a needlessly improbable route.
\item \emph{Do not dilate volatilities uniformly by default.} Shocking part of
the severity through dependence is both more faithful to observed crises and
less improbable at equal severity.
\item \emph{Set cross-market correlations from a coupling map.} The
decomposition \eqref{eq:blocks} says which segments stress ties together and
by how much, in a common unit.
\item \emph{Run a placebo null} before adopting any crisis indicator based on
estimated moments.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Few results survive.} Only coupling under stress passes both nulls
on every panel. Jumps, relaxation and sign are not established.
\item \emph{Return periods are not validated.} The severity ladder is about
twice too mild compared with history (table~\ref{tab:ladder}). Only relative
comparisons (overpayment, composition) withstand this flaw.
\item \emph{H8 is retained only against the i.i.d.\ null.} It serves only to
calibrate a composition and should not be used to tell crisis types apart.
\item \emph{Nothing here forecasts.} The measures are diagnostic.
\item \emph{Shape is estimated asset by asset.} Non-linear tail dependence is
left out of $\JJ$, which therefore understates the true divergence, especially
under stress.
\item \emph{Cross-sections are small.} Twenty stocks or 48 industries are not
enough to make the figures presented constants; a scenario radius is not
comparable from one asset universe to another.
\item \emph{The entropic CISS is incomplete.} Its money-market segment awaits
Euribor, and its comparisons rest on three recessions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

If the Gaussian is the distribution of maximal ignorance, any departure from it
measures information, in nats. This idea gives an exact decomposition of a
market's uncertainty into three channels, and a unit-free index, $\JJ$, that
measures the distance from maximal ignorance.

The empirical result is first a warning. Every prediction of the framework is
visible in the raw data: the index rises under stress, it moves as a sawtooth,
its jumps are one-sided and come with negative skewness. Rerun on data whose
time order has been shuffled, almost all of it disappears, and several effects
are even stronger in the shuffled data. Only one thing durably separates a
crisis from a run of large days: markets couple.

For institutions that design stress scenarios, the framework offers three
tools. A unit, the nat, that measures a scenario's distance from the current
state of the market and reads in standard deviations. An audit, which shows
that classical scenarios pay $15$ to $33\,\%$ too much for their tail loss,
because they dilate volatilities uniformly instead of coupling markets as
crises do. And a map, the entropic CISS, that says which segments stress ties
together: a complement to the CISS, not a predictor.

\section{The bridge from order flow to Gaussian prices}
\label{app:pont}

Over an interval, let $V$ be traded volume and $Q$ net signed order flow (buys
minus sells). Two assumptions suffice. \emph{Linear impact}: the log-price
change equals $\lambda Q$, as in \citet{kyle1985}. \emph{Variance proportional
to volume}: given $V$, the market knows only the mean of $Q$ and a variance
$\varsigma^2V$. Maximum entropy then gives
\begin{equation}
\Delta X \mid V \;\sim\; \mathcal N\!\left(\lambda m,\ \lambda^2\varsigma^2 V\right).
\end{equation}
The conditioning variable is volume, not time: this is the
mixture-of-distributions hypothesis of \citet{clark1973} and
\citet{tauchen1983}, and \citet{ane2000} show that returns measured in
transaction time are close to Gaussian. Unconditionally, the return is a
mixture of Gaussians, hence fat-tailed \citep{mandelbrot1967}: fat tails alone
do not prove that information is present. If, in addition, increments are
independent in the volume clock, the log-price is a time-changed Brownian
motion and the price a geometric Brownian motion. Both assumptions are
imperfect (actual impact is closer to a square root, and order flow is
autocorrelated), which is why all empirical work is done in calendar time.

\section{The dynamics of the index: a theorem and two postulates}
\label{app:dynamique}

\paragraph{The theorem.} For a return $X$ and a signal $Y$,
$\mathbb{E}_Y[h(X\mid Y)]=h(X)-I(X;Y)\le h(X)$ \citep{cover2006}. Receiving
information lowers entropy \emph{on average} by exactly the mutual
information. A particular realisation can raise it (a confusing signal); the
jump component is therefore mainly, not exclusively, one-sided.

\paragraph{Postulate 1 (relaxation).} Absent new information, $\JJ$ returns to
a long-run level: $d\JJ_t=-\kappa(\JJ_t-\bar\JJ)\,dt+\sigma_\JJ\,dW_t$. An
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process is the only process that is Markov, Gaussian and
stationary at once \citep{doob1942}. Adding the jumps caused by news gives
\begin{equation}
d\JJ_t \;=\; -\kappa(\JJ_t-\bar\JJ)\,dt \;+\; \sigma_\JJ\,dW_t \;+\; dZ_t ,
\end{equation}
where $Z_t$ is the sum of positive jumps up to $t$: the class of
\citet{bns2001}. The half-life of a shock is $\ln2/\kappa$.

\paragraph{Postulate 2 (sign).} $\JJ$ is an even function of skewness: a
distribution with a long left tail (a crash) and its mirror image (a rally)
have the same $\JJ$. To say anything about direction, an outside ingredient is
needed. We postulate that jumps come with negative skewness, relying on the
leverage effect \citep{black1976,engle1993}.

\paragraph{Why dependence and tails should rise together.} Important news hits
every asset on the same day. On that day assets move together (coupling rises)
and each records an unusual loss (its tails thicken). The two channels
describe the same day seen from two places. In calm markets, large moves are
asset-specific and couple nothing. This is H6. The null shows, however, that
this coincidence is also produced mechanically by estimation.

\section{Empirical details}
\label{app:details}

\paragraph{Estimators.} Two covariance estimators run in parallel: a 252-day
rolling window with the \citet{ledoit2004} correction, and exponential
weighting with a 60-day half-life and $10\,\%$ shrinkage towards the diagonal.
The second prevents an extreme day \emph{leaving} the window from creating a
spurious jump. The shape deficit uses the \citet{hyvarinen1998} approximation,
whose two terms separate a skewness channel and a tail channel; the
better-known Edgeworth expansion $S^2/12+K^2/48$ overstates the deficit of a
Student law with 4 degrees of freedom by a factor of 69. Jumps are flagged at
four times a local bipower-variation scale lagged by one day \citep{bns2004}.

\paragraph{Inference.} Two consecutive rolling windows share 251 of their 252
days. Regime comparisons therefore use a 252-day circular block bootstrap. The
relaxation $p$-value follows the Dickey--Fuller distribution. Forecasts (H7)
are re-estimated each year on the past, compared on dates 21 days apart, with
the \citet{dm1995} test.

\paragraph{Raw stress-regime results (S\&P~500).} Under stress ($1,076$ dates
against $6,481$), scale rises by $5.97$~nats, dependence falls by $0.99$, shape
rises by $0.27$ ($p=0.16$), $\JJ$ rises by $1.94$ ($p<0.001$, but without
passing the nulls). Average correlation goes from $0.26$ to $0.32$, the
effective number of modes from $13.1$ to $11.4$. Market skewness does not
change significantly between regimes: the skewness signature is a property of
jumps, not of the regime. The index shares about a fifth of its variance with
volatility ($r=0.46$), and the shape channel is orthogonal to it ($r=0.08$).

\section{Essential proofs}
\label{app:preuves}

\paragraph{Gaussian entropy and equation~\eqref{eq:three}.} For
$q=\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)$, $h(q)=\tfrac12\log\bigl((2\pi e)^N\det\Sigma\bigr)$.
With $\Sigma=DRD$, $\det\Sigma=(\prod_i\sigma_i)^2\det R$, hence
$h(q)=\tfrac N2\log(2\pi e)+\sum_i\log\sigma_i+\tfrac12\log\det R$. Since
$\det R$ is the product of the eigenvalues of $R$, which sum to $N$, the
arithmetic--geometric mean inequality gives $\det R\le1$: correlation always
destroys entropy.

\paragraph{The deficit is a divergence.} If $q$ is the Gaussian with the mean
and covariance of $p$, then $\mathbb{E}_p[\log q]=\mathbb{E}_q[\log q]$ because
$\log q$ is quadratic. Hence $\KL{p}{q}=h(q)-h(p)=\DD\ge0$, with equality if and
only if $p$ is Gaussian.

\paragraph{The structural index is a divergence.} With $q_i$ the Gaussians
with each asset's mean and volatility,
$\KL{p}{\prod_i q_i}=-h(p)+\sum_i\log\sigma_i+\tfrac N2\log(2\pi e)$.
Substituting \eqref{eq:three} for $h(p)$ leaves $\DD-\tfrac12\log\det R
=\DD+C=\JJ$.

\paragraph{Mixing compositions.} If $w^\top\Sigma_aw=w^\top\Sigma_bw=s^2$, then
for every $\alpha\in[0,1]$,
$w^\top\bigl((1-\alpha)\Sigma_a+\alpha\Sigma_b\bigr)w=s^2$, by linearity.

\paragraph{The price of a directional shock.} For two Gaussians with the same
covariance, $\KL{p_1}{p_0}=\tfrac12\Delta^\top\Sigma^{-1}\Delta$ where $\Delta$
is the difference in means. A shock of $k$ standard deviations on a portfolio
therefore costs $k^2/2$~nats, and $\eta$~nats buy $\sqrt{2\eta}$ standard
deviations.

\paragraph{Decomposition by segment.} Applying the definition of $C$ to each
block,
\[C(R)-\textstyle\sum_kC(R_{kk})=\tfrac12\bigl(\sum_k\log\det R_{kk}-\log\det R\bigr),\]
which is the Gaussian mutual information between blocks, non-negative by
Fischer's inequality.
